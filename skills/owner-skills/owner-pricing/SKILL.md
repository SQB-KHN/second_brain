# Pricing Review and Change Plan

Most prices were set once, by adding a percentage to cost, and have moved since only when a supplier forced it. Meanwhile costs rose unevenly, what you sell changed, and a discount somebody gave a large customer in 2022 is still being applied. A five percent price rise is usually worth more than a year of cost-cutting, and it is the decision owners defer longest.

## When to use this

Trigger on: pricing, price increase, price list, rate card, margin, discount policy, repricing, price review, competitor pricing, "how much should I charge", "am I charging enough", "should I raise prices".

## Before you start

Required: current prices, and what it costs you to deliver each thing.

Ask, briefly:
1. **When prices last changed, and by how much.** A business that has not moved prices in three years while costs rose is the commonest case, and the size of the correction usually surprises the owner.
2. Cost movement since then, by input, because costs rarely move uniformly.
3. **What your customers actually compare you against**, which is often not the competitor you think, and is sometimes doing nothing at all.
4. Where you win and lose deals, and whether price is the stated reason. The stated reason and the real reason differ.
5. What discounts are being given, by whom, and whether anybody approves them.

## Workflow

### 1. Do the arithmetic that changes the conversation, first

Compute in code, never in prose, and show the working:

- The margin impact of a price change at current volume
- **The volume you could afford to lose and still be better off.** This is the number that unlocks the decision. On a 30 percent margin, a 10 percent price rise means you can lose roughly a quarter of your volume and be no worse off, and almost no owner knows that before it is computed.
- The cost increase you have absorbed since the last change, as a percentage
- **What a discount actually costs.** A 10 percent discount on a 30 percent margin gives away a third of the profit on that sale, and it is routinely handed over as though it were a small concession.

This section changes more pricing decisions than any argument about value.

### 2. Separate the three problems that all look like pricing

- **Prices are too low for the value delivered.** Fix the price.
- **Costs rose and prices did not.** Fix the price, and note this is a correction rather than an increase, which matters for how it gets communicated.
- **The margin is poor because of what happens after the sale**: rework, over-servicing, slow payment. **A price rise does not fix this and may make it worse.**

Say which of the three you have. Raising prices to solve an operational problem produces an angry customer and the same problem.

### 3. Price against value where you can, and cost only where you cannot

Cost-plus is the default because it is easy, and it means your prices are set by your supplier rather than by your customer.

Where possible work from what the customer gets: what it saves them, what it earns them, what it costs them not to have it, and what the alternative costs. Where that cannot be established, say so, use cost-plus, and name what would establish it.

### 4. Build a price architecture rather than a single number

- Differentiate where customers genuinely differ: volume, service level, speed, payment terms, risk
- **Offer a smaller option so the main one is a choice rather than a threshold.** Many owners have exactly one price, so every conversation becomes yes or no.
- Make the difference between tiers something the customer values, rather than something that costs you more to deliver

### 5. Fix the discount problem, which is usually bigger than the price problem

- Who can give a discount, and up to what
- **What must be given in return:** volume, a longer term, faster payment, a reference, a smaller scope
- Which existing discounts no longer have a reason behind them. These are the easiest margin recovery available and nobody looks.

State the total annual value of discounts currently being given. That number is usually the finding.

### 6. Plan the change rather than only deciding it

- **Who moves first:** new customers, then renewals, then existing. Existing last, and never all at once.
- Notice period, and what is honoured at the old price
- **Whether to grandfather anybody, and for how long.** Permanent grandfathering creates two price lists forever.
- **What you will do when the largest customer objects, decided before they do**
- The signal to watch, and the point at which you would pause

### 7. Say what would make this a mistake

Be specific: a contract that fixes your price, a customer whose loss you could not absorb, a competitor about to undercut, a market where price is genuinely the only differentiator. Where one applies, say so plainly.

## Output format

**1. The arithmetic.** Margin impact, volume you can afford to lose, cost absorbed since the last change, current annual cost of discounts.

**2. Which problem you have.** Underpriced, cost-driven, or operational.

**3. Value basis**, where it can be established, and what would establish it where it cannot.

**4. Proposed price architecture.** Tiers, what differentiates them, what each is worth.

**5. Discount policy.** Who, how much, in return for what, and which existing ones to end.

**6. Change plan.** Sequence, notice, grandfathering, and the response to the largest objection.

**7. What to watch**, and the point at which to pause.

**8. What would make this a mistake.**

## Quality bar

- Is the volume you could afford to lose computed and stated
- Is the annual cost of current discounts totalled in money
- Is it stated which of the three problems this is, before any price is proposed
- Is a value basis attempted before falling back to cost-plus
- Does the architecture differentiate on something customers value rather than on what costs you more
- Does the discount policy state what must be given in return
- Does the change plan sequence customers rather than moving everyone at once
- Is the response to the largest customer's objection decided in advance
- Is there a stated point at which to pause
- Is there an honest section on what would make this a mistake

## What this skill does not do

- It does not set your prices. It does the arithmetic, shows the options, and says what each risks.
- It does not know your market, your competitors' real prices, or what your customers will accept. It works with what you tell it and marks the rest as assumption.
- It does not predict how many customers you will lose. It tells you how many you could afford to.
- **It does not advise on competition law.** Anything involving coordinating prices with competitors, or terms that could be seen as anti-competitive, goes to a lawyer.
- It does not write the price increase message. Pair it with the commercial conversation skill for that.
