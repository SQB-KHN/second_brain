# Pricing Review and Change Plan

Does the pricing arithmetic owners never do, separates a pricing problem from a cost or operational one, and plans the change with a sequence and an answer ready for the largest customer.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who sets a price and lives with it: SME owners and founders, agency and consultancy owners, manufacturers and traders, retail, restaurant, clinic and gym owners, professional practice owners, distributors, and freelancers deciding what to charge next year.

## The problem it solves

Most prices were set once, by adding a percentage to cost, and have moved since only when a supplier forced it.

Meanwhile costs rose unevenly, what you sell changed, and a discount somebody gave a large customer in 2022 is still being applied because nobody reviews discounts. The result is a business working harder each year for the same money, where the owner is looking for savings.

**A five percent price rise is usually worth more than a year of cost-cutting**, and it is the decision owners defer longest. Not from ignorance, but because the arithmetic that would make it obvious never gets done, and without it the risk feels unbounded. Losing customers is a vivid fear; the number of customers you could afford to lose is not a number anybody has.

---

## What you give it

**Minimum:** current prices, and what it costs you to deliver each thing.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **When prices last changed, and by how much** | Three years of absorbed cost increases is the commonest case, and the size of the correction usually surprises |
| Cost movement since then, by input | Costs rarely move uniformly, so a flat rise is usually the wrong shape |
| **What customers actually compare you against** | Often not the competitor you think, and sometimes doing nothing at all |
| Where you win and lose, and the stated reason | The stated reason and the real reason differ |
| **What discounts are being given, by whom** | The total is usually the finding |

## What you get back

1. **The arithmetic**: margin impact, the volume you could afford to lose, cost absorbed since the last change, and what discounts cost you per year
2. **Which of the three problems you have**: underpriced, cost-driven, or operational
3. A value basis where one can be established, and what would establish it where it cannot
4. **A price architecture**: tiers, what differentiates them, what each is worth
5. **Discount policy**: who, how much, in return for what, and which existing ones to end
6. **A change plan**: sequence, notice, grandfathering, and the response to the largest objection
7. What to watch, and the point at which to pause
8. What would make this a mistake

## The number that unlocks the decision

**The volume you could afford to lose and still be better off.**

On a 30 percent margin, a 10 percent price rise means you can lose roughly a quarter of your volume and be no worse off. Almost no owner knows that before it is computed, and it changes the conversation completely, because the fear is losing customers and the arithmetic says how many you can afford to.

The same section computes what a discount actually costs. **A 10 percent discount on a 30 percent margin gives away a third of the profit on that sale**, and it is routinely handed over as though it were a small concession. Seeing the annual total of discounts currently being given is often the single most useful output here.

## Three problems that all look like pricing

| The problem | The fix |
|---|---|
| **Prices are too low for the value delivered** | Fix the price |
| **Costs rose and prices did not** | Fix the price, but call it a correction. It changes how you communicate it |
| **Margin is poor because of what happens after the sale** | Rework, over-servicing, slow payment. **A price rise does not fix this and may make it worse** |

It says which one you have before proposing any price. Raising prices to solve an operational problem produces an angry customer and the same problem.

## Cost-plus means your supplier sets your prices

Cost-plus is the default because it is easy. The consequence is that your prices are set by whoever sells to you, not by what your work is worth to the customer.

Where it can, this works from what the customer gets: what it saves them, what it earns them, what it costs them not to have it, and what the alternative costs. Where that genuinely cannot be established, it says so, uses cost-plus, and names what would establish it later.

## The change plan is the part that gets skipped

Deciding to raise prices takes an afternoon. Doing it without losing your three largest customers takes a plan:

- **New customers first, then renewals, then existing.** Never everyone at once.
- Notice period, and what is honoured at the old price
- **Whether to grandfather anybody, and for how long.** Permanent grandfathering creates two price lists forever, and nobody ever removes the second one.
- **What you say when the largest customer objects, decided before they do**
- The signal to watch, and the point at which you would pause

## What good output looks like

A strong review is uncomfortable in the arithmetic section and reassuring immediately afterwards. The cost you have absorbed since the last change is usually larger than expected, and the volume you could afford to lose is usually larger too.

The discount total is the other reliable surprise. Most owners have never added it up, and it is frequently a bigger number than the price rise being considered.

If the recommendation is not to raise prices, that is a real finding rather than a failure. Where the problem turns out to be operational, or where a contract fixes your price, or where a customer's loss could not be absorbed, it says so plainly in the "what would make this a mistake" section.

## Using it

Give it your prices, your costs, and when you last changed anything.

Examples that work:

- "Have not raised prices in three years. Costs are up about 18 percent. What can I do and what would it be worth?"
- "How much volume can I afford to lose if I put prices up 8 percent?"
- "My team gives discounts without asking. What is that costing me a year?"
- "One customer is 40 percent of turnover and pays the least. How do I handle this?"
- "Should I have three price tiers or one? I currently have one."

Telling it what discounts are actually being given, rather than what the policy says, produces the most useful answer.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not set your prices. It does the arithmetic, shows the options, and says what each risks.
- It does not know your market, your competitors' real prices, or what your customers will accept. It works with what you tell it and marks the rest as assumption.
- It does not predict how many customers you will lose. It tells you how many you could afford to.
- **It does not advise on competition law.** Anything involving coordinating prices with competitors, or terms that could look anti-competitive, goes to a lawyer.
- It does not write the price increase message. Pair it with the commercial conversation skill for that.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The rise it suggests looks large | You absorbed several years of cost increases | Check the absorbed-cost figure. It is usually the explanation. |
| It says do not raise prices | The problem is operational, not pricing | Rework and over-servicing do not improve with a higher price. |
| Discount total higher than expected | Nobody had added it up before | That is the finding, and usually the easiest money to recover. |
| It will not predict lost customers | It cannot, and neither can anybody | You get how many you could afford to lose, which is actionable. |
| Tiers feel like extra work | One price makes every conversation yes or no | A smaller option turns a threshold into a choice. |
| No message drafted | Different skill | Pair it with the commercial conversation skill. |
