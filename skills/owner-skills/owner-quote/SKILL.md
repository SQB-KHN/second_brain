# Quotation and Scope

The quote is a number and a one-line description, sent quickly because the customer asked for it quickly. Then the job runs, the customer expects three things that were never priced, and there is no document to point at. So either the margin goes or the relationship does, and usually it is the margin.

## When to use this

Trigger on: quotation, quote, estimate, proposal, scope of work, SOW, tender, rate card, commercial proposal, "how much should I charge for this", "they asked for a quote", "pricing this job".

## Before you start

Required: what the customer asked for, and what it will take to deliver.

Ask, briefly:
1. **What is being asked for, in the customer's own words, and what they have not said.** Most scope disputes come from something both sides assumed differently and neither wrote down.
2. What it costs you to deliver, including the parts people forget: revisions, coordination, travel, waiting, the meetings, and the person who has to stay available.
3. Your terms: payment schedule, validity, and what happens when scope changes.
4. Whether you are competing on price, and roughly against whom.
5. **What you would not do at any price**, and what you would want in return for a discount.

## Workflow

### 1. Price the work, not the deliverable

The commonest underquoting error is pricing the visible output and none of the work around it. List the whole delivery:

- The work itself
- Revisions, and how many are included
- Coordination, meetings and reporting time
- Waiting time and dependencies on the customer
- Travel and site time
- Handover, documentation and training
- The period afterwards when you will still be answering questions

Price these or exclude them explicitly. **An unpriced expectation is a discount you did not decide to give.**

### 2. Define scope by its boundary, not by its content

A scope written as a list of what is included leaves everything unmentioned ambiguous. A scope written with an explicit boundary does not.

For each element: what is included, what is not, and what would make it a change. Write the exclusions plainly. They are not rude; they are the part that protects the relationship when something comes up.

### 3. Write the assumptions down, each with the consequence if it is wrong

The document that saves a job is the assumptions list. Content will be supplied by the tenth. Site access is available in working hours. Approvals come from one person. The existing system works as described.

**Every assumption gets a consequence:** if this is not true, the timeline moves by X or the cost changes by Y. That converts a future argument into a clause somebody already agreed to.

### 4. Set the payment schedule to match your risk

- Advance, especially with a new customer or where you buy materials first
- Milestones tied to something verifiable, not to a date
- **Final payment tied to delivery, not to the customer's internal process.** "On acceptance" with no definition of acceptance is an open-ended term.
- A validity period on the quote, because your costs move

State what happens on late payment, once, plainly.

### 5. Handle a change of scope before it happens

A change process in the quote is worth more than any negotiation afterwards: what counts as a change, how it will be priced, and whether work stops or continues while it is agreed.

Without it, every change becomes a conversation about goodwill, and goodwill is the thing you run out of on the third change.

### 6. Present price so it can be discussed without collapsing

- Break it into components the customer can see, so a reduction becomes a decision about scope rather than about your margin
- Where you offer options, make them genuinely different in what they deliver, not only in price
- **Do not lead with the discounted price.** A price you open below is a price you will be pushed below.

State the minimum you would accept **before you send it**, and what you would want in exchange. Deciding that now is the difference between a negotiation and a retreat.

### 7. Say what you need from them

Every quote should name what the customer has to provide and by when. It establishes that delivery is joint, and it is the first thing you point at when a timeline slips for their reason.

## Output format

**1. What we understood you need.** In their words, with anything ambiguous named.

**2. Scope.** Included, and explicitly not included.

**3. Assumptions**, each with the consequence if it is wrong.

**4. Price**, broken into components.

**5. Options**, where offered, genuinely different in delivery.

**6. Timeline**, with what depends on the customer.

**7. Payment schedule, and validity.**

**8. Change process.**

**9. What we need from you**, and by when.

**10. Internal note, not for sending.** Your floor, what you would trade for it, and what you priced that they may not expect.

## Quality bar

- Is the work priced rather than the deliverable, including revisions, coordination and waiting time
- Is scope written with an explicit boundary, so exclusions are stated rather than implied
- Does every assumption carry the consequence of it being wrong
- Are payment milestones tied to something verifiable, rather than to a date or an undefined acceptance
- Is there a change process, defined before any change happens
- Is the price broken into components that can be reduced by removing scope
- Are options genuinely different in what they deliver, not only in price
- Is a validity period stated
- Does the quote name what the customer must provide and by when
- Is the floor decided before sending, and kept in the internal note rather than in the quote

## What this skill does not do

- It does not know your costs. It structures the quote around the figures you supply and flags what is usually forgotten.
- It does not set your price. It shows what you are pricing and what you have left unpriced.
- It is not a contract, and its terms are commercial rather than legal. Anything that has to be enforceable goes to a lawyer.
- It does not tell you whether you will win the work.
- It does not negotiate for you, and the internal note is never part of what gets sent.
