# Quotation and Scope

Builds a quote that prices the whole job rather than the visible deliverable, bounds the scope explicitly, and defines the change process before any change happens.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who quotes for work: agency and consultancy owners, contractors and builders, manufacturers taking custom orders, service businesses, professional practice owners, freelancers and solo consultants, event and interiors businesses, and anyone whose customers ask "how much" before anything is written down.

## The problem it solves

The quote is a number and a one-line description, sent quickly because the customer asked for it quickly.

Then the job runs. The customer expects three things that were never priced, because they were never excluded either. There is no document to point at, so the conversation becomes about goodwill rather than about what was agreed. Either the margin goes or the relationship does, and it is usually the margin, because the relationship is worth more.

The underlying error is pricing the deliverable instead of the work. The visible output is priced; the revisions, the coordination calls, the waiting, the travel, the handover and the month afterwards of answering questions are not. **Every one of those is a discount you did not decide to give.**

Writing a quote properly takes twenty minutes longer and prevents the whole sequence. It does not get done because under time pressure a number feels like enough.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** what the customer asked for, and what it will take to deliver.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What they asked for in their own words, and what they did not say** | Most scope disputes come from something both sides assumed differently |
| What delivery actually costs you | Including revisions, coordination, waiting, travel, and staying available afterwards |
| Your terms | Payment schedule, validity, and what happens when scope changes |
| Whether you are competing on price | Changes how the price is presented, not what it is |
| **What you would not do at any price** | And what you would want in return for a discount |

## What you get back

1. What we understood you need, in their words, with anything ambiguous named
2. **Scope, with an explicit boundary**: included, and expressly not included
3. **Assumptions, each with the consequence if it is wrong**
4. Price broken into components
5. Options where offered, genuinely different in what they deliver
6. Timeline, with what depends on the customer
7. Payment schedule and validity
8. **A change process, defined before any change happens**
9. What we need from you, and by when
10. **An internal note, not for sending**: your floor, what you would trade for it, and what you priced that they may not expect

---

## Scope defined by its boundary

A scope written as a list of what is included leaves everything unmentioned ambiguous, and ambiguity always resolves in the customer's favour because they are the one paying.

So every element gets three things: what is included, what is not, and what would make it a change. The exclusions are written plainly. **They are not rude; they are the part that protects the relationship when something comes up**, because the alternative is refusing something verbally with no document behind you.

## Assumptions with consequences attached

The document that saves a job is the assumptions list, and the thing that makes it work is the consequence.

| Assumption | Consequence if wrong |
|---|---|
| Content supplied by the 10th | Delivery moves day for day |
| Site access during working hours | Out-of-hours work charged at the stated rate |
| Approvals come from one person | Each additional approver adds a review cycle |
| The existing system works as described | Investigation billed separately, at cost |

An assumption without a consequence is a hope. An assumption with one converts a future argument into a clause somebody already agreed to.

## Payment tied to something verifiable

Milestones tied to dates fail when either side slips. Milestones tied to "acceptance" fail when acceptance is undefined, which it usually is.

**"On acceptance" with no definition of acceptance is an open-ended term**, and it is how small businesses end up chasing final payments for months. Every schedule here ties payment to something you can point at, plus a validity period on the quote, because your costs move and a quote from four months ago is not a price.

## The internal note

The last section never goes to the customer. It carries your floor, what you would want in exchange for reaching it, and anything you priced that they may not have expected.

Deciding the floor **before sending** is the difference between a negotiation and a retreat. A price you have not set a floor under is one you will discover the floor of during the call.

## What good output looks like

A strong quote has an exclusions list that feels slightly uncomfortable to send. That discomfort is the point, and it is a fraction of the discomfort of the conversation you would otherwise have in week three.

The price should be broken into components, so that when the customer asks for a reduction the conversation is about removing scope rather than about your margin. A single total invites a single question.

If the quote comes back higher than you expected, look at what was priced that you had been absorbing. That gap, between the price and what you would have quoted, is usually the margin you have been giving away.

## Using it

Say what they asked for and what it takes to deliver.

Examples that work:

- "Client wants a website redesign. Here is the brief. Two rounds of revisions included, no content writing."
- "Fabrication job, 40 units, they supply the drawings. What am I forgetting to price?"
- "This customer always asks for extras. Write the quote so that conversation is easier."
- "Same job as last time but they want it in three weeks instead of six. Reprice it."
- "I keep losing money on projects like this. Show me what I am not pricing."

That last one usually produces the most useful answer.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not know your costs. It structures the quote around your figures and flags what is usually forgotten.
- It does not set your price. It shows what you are pricing and what you have left unpriced.
- **It is not a contract**, and its terms are commercial rather than legal. Anything that has to be enforceable goes to a lawyer.
- It does not tell you whether you will win the work.
- It does not negotiate for you, and the internal note is never part of what gets sent.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Quote higher than you would have sent | It priced what you were absorbing | That gap is the margin you have been giving away. |
| Exclusions feel blunt | They are written plainly on purpose | A vague scope always resolves in the customer's favour. |
| It asks what you would not do | It is building your floor before you need it | Answer it now. During the call is too late. |
| Options look too different | They are meant to differ in delivery, not price | Options that differ only in price are a discount ladder. |
| No single total at the top | Components invite scope conversations | Ask for a total as well. The components stay. |
| Internal note appeared in the draft | It is a separate section by design | It is never part of what gets sent. Check before forwarding. |
