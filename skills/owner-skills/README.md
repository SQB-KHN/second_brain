# Business Owners Skills for Claude

Five skills for the commercial decisions an owner makes alone: what actually makes money, what you are signing, what you charge, and the four conversations everybody avoids.

They run inside your own Claude account, on your own sales files and documents. Nothing to install on your machine, nothing to connect, no coding.

---

## Who these are for

People who own the whole result: founders of small and medium businesses, SME proprietors in manufacturing, trading and distribution, D2C and ecommerce founders, agency and consultancy owners, restaurant, retail, clinic and gym owners, professional practice owners, distributors and dealers, freelancers and solo consultants scaling up, and second-generation family business managers.

The thing that defines this audience is not the size of the business. It is that **there is no CFO, no lawyer and no HR head to check you.** Four of the five need no export from any system at all.

## The line none of these cross

**No advice that needs a licence.**

Nothing here gives legal, tax, accounting, employment-law or regulatory advice. It explains what something means in plain language, names what to ask about, and says who to ask.

This matters more for an owner than for anybody else in the library, and for a specific reason. A finance professional reading a skill has a CA to check it against. A salesperson has a manager. **An owner reading this may have nobody**, which makes the skill the most authoritative thing in the room, and that is exactly when its limits have to be loudest.

So the contract review will not tell you whether a clause is enforceable. The pricing skill will not touch anything that looks like coordinating with competitors. The conversation skill will not draft a legal notice, and says when you have reached the point of needing one. In each case it does the genuinely useful thing, which is to make an hour of professional time cheap by handing over three specific questions rather than fourteen pages.

## Why these five

**Where the money comes from.** The business is profitable, so it must be working. Underneath, some products lose money on every sale and two customers subsidise the rest, and a P&L never asks that question.

**Contract review.** Fourteen pages, work starts Monday, and knowing which clauses matter is exactly what nobody teaches an owner. The clause that mattered surfaces two years later, when the leverage has gone.

**Quotation.** A number and a one-line description, sent quickly. Then the customer expects three things that were never priced, and there is nothing to point at. **Pricing.** A five percent rise is usually worth more than a year of cost-cutting, and it is the decision deferred longest, because the arithmetic that would make it obvious never gets done.

**Difficult conversations.** Four of them decide more profit than anything else, and all four get deferred until they are emotional rather than commercial.

## They work together

Each stands alone. In sequence they cover the commercial year:

```
Sales and costs      →  Where the Money Comes From  →  what to stop, keep and reprice
Prices and costs     →  Pricing Review              →  the rise, and what you can afford to lose
The decision         →  Difficult Conversations     →  the message, and the answer to the pushback
A new job            →  Quotation and Scope         →  priced whole, bounded, with a change process
Before you sign      →  Contract Review             →  what you would actually be agreeing to
```

Three pairings worth knowing. Run the profitability analysis before the pricing review, because it tells you which customers and products the rise should actually apply to. The pricing review then hands its decision to the conversation skill, which writes the message. And the quote's terms are the ones the contract review will later be checking, so getting them right once saves the argument twice.

These sit downstream of the sales and finance work: pipeline and forecasting belong with sales, cash flow and management reporting with finance. What lands here is the commercial decision itself, the part nobody else was allowed to touch.

## What makes these different from asking directly

**Contribution before profit.** Dropping a product removes its contribution and leaves the fixed costs exactly where they were. Allocating rent across products makes something look unprofitable when it is helping pay for the roof, and owners close working product lines over this every year.

**Ranked by total, never by percentage.** A 60 percent margin on something sold twice a year is worth less than 18 percent on something sold daily, and ranking by percentage is the commonest way an owner drops the wrong product.

**The volume you could afford to lose.** On a 30 percent margin, a 10 percent price rise means losing roughly a quarter of your volume leaves you no worse off. Almost nobody knows that before it is computed, and it is the number that unlocks the decision.

**What a discount actually costs.** A 10 percent discount on a 30 percent margin gives away a third of the profit on that sale. The annual total of discounts currently being given is usually the single most useful figure in a pricing review.

**Three problems that all look like pricing.** Underpriced, cost-driven, or an operational problem after the sale. **A price rise does not fix the third and may make it worse**, so it says which one you have before proposing a number.

**Missing terms reported before present ones, and unusual separated from merely unfavourable.** Absence is invisible when you read a contract straight through: you notice bad clauses, not the one that is not there. And arguing a standard term rarely moves, while costing you credibility on the items that would have.

**Scope bounded rather than listed, and every assumption carries its consequence.** A scope written as what is included leaves everything unmentioned ambiguous, and ambiguity resolves in favour of whoever is paying. "Content by the tenth" is a hope; "content by the tenth, or delivery moves day for day" is a clause somebody already agreed to.

**All three responses decided before you send, and the channel recommended rather than assumed.** An exception given for nothing becomes the new price, for that customer and everyone they tell. And ending a long relationship should be a call: a well-written email for a conversation that needed a phone call has done harm.

## Getting started

1. Take one skill's folder and compress it to a `.zip`. The file inside must stay named `SKILL.md`, because that is what Claude looks for. The guide beside it is not needed and can be left out.
2. Go to **Customize**, then **Skills**, then add a skill and upload the zip.
3. Repeat for each skill you want.

Only the profitability analysis and the pricing review use code execution. The other three need nothing, so most of this set runs on a free plan. Skills you upload stay private to your account, and once installed they work in Chat and Cowork.

**Where to start.** Run the profitability analysis on a year of sales. It reliably names something you were protecting, usually a large customer whose contribution turns out to be near zero once discounts and servicing are netted off.

## Using them

Upload the file or paste the document, and describe it the way you would to a friend who runs a business.

- "A year of sales by product and customer. Materials and freight vary. What is losing money?"
- "Client MSA, we are the supplier, worth 30 lakh a year. What should I raise?"
- "Client wants a website redesign. Two rounds of revisions, no content writing. Quote it."
- "Have not raised prices in three years. Costs up 18 percent. What can I do?"
- "Invoice is 74 days overdue and I have sent two polite reminders. What now?"

## What these will not do

- **No legal, tax, accounting, employment or regulatory advice.** They explain what something means and name who to ask. The contract review will not say whether a clause is enforceable.
- **No decisions made for you.** These size a decision, show what each option costs, and hand it back. Dropping a customer, signing, repricing and sending all stay with you.
- **No competition law.** Anything involving coordinating prices with competitors, or terms that could look anti-competitive, goes to a lawyer.
- **No legal notices or demand letters.** The conversation skill says when you have reached the point of needing one.
- **No invented numbers and no prediction.** No market prices, competitor rates or benchmark margins; what you do not supply becomes an assumption. It will not say how many customers you will lose, whether you will win the work, or whether a rise will be accepted.
- **No system access, and nothing sent.** They read files and documents you provide, never connecting to your accounting system, and every output is a draft for you to check and own.

## What you need

- A Claude account, with code execution turned on for two of the five
- Your sales file in Excel or CSV, and documents pasted in
- Nothing else. No installation, no terminal, no coding, nothing to connect
