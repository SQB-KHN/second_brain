# Contract and Commercial Terms Review

Reads an agreement and tells you in plain language what each significant term means for you commercially, what is missing, what is unusual, and which three things are worth a lawyer's time.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who signs commercial agreements without a legal team behind them: SME owners and founders, agency and consultancy owners, professional practice owners, distributors and dealers, contractors and suppliers, freelancers taking on client work, and anyone about to renew something they signed years ago.

## The problem it solves

The contract arrives as a PDF, it is fourteen pages, and the work starts on Monday.

Reading it properly means knowing which clauses matter and which are standard, which is exactly the thing nobody teaches an owner. A lawyer would tell you in twenty minutes, but engaging one for every vendor agreement and every renewal is not realistic, so most get signed on the assumption that they are normal.

The clause that mattered then surfaces two years later, in a dispute or at renewal, when the leverage has gone. Usually it is one of three: an auto-renewal window that closed silently, an uncapped liability nobody noticed, or a payment term tied to an "acceptance" that was never defined.

This does the reading. It is not a lawyer, and it is clear about that, but it tells you what you would be signing and which parts genuinely warrant paying for advice.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the agreement, or the terms in question.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Which side you are on** | The same clause is protective or dangerous depending on which end of it you sit |
| What the deal is worth, and over what period | A clause worth arguing on a fifty lakh contract is not worth the relationship on a fifty thousand one |
| **What matters most to you** | Getting paid, being able to leave, protecting what you own, or capping what you could owe. Review effort goes there first |
| Whether it is negotiable | Negotiable produces a negotiation list. Take it or leave it produces a risk list |
| **Anything agreed verbally** | The commonest contract problem is not a bad clause but a missing one |

## What you get back

1. A verdict: what is worth raising before signing, in two or three lines
2. **What is missing**, reported before what is present
3. Money terms, each in plain language with what it means for your cash
4. **Exit terms**: notice, cost, the auto-renewal window, what survives
5. Risk terms: liability, indemnity, penalties, insurance
6. Ownership and confidentiality
7. **Unusual separated from unfavourable but standard**
8. A ranked list to raise: must change, worth asking, unlikely, accept
9. **For a lawyer**: the specific items and why
10. What the review could not assess

---

## The line it will not cross

**This is not legal advice and it is not a substitute for a lawyer.**

It reads the document, explains what each significant term means for you commercially, and flags what is unusual. That is genuinely useful and it is a different thing from advice.

Where a term's consequences depend on jurisdiction, statute or case law, it says so and stops. **It will not tell you whether a clause is enforceable, and it will not tell you what your rights are.** What it will do is make the lawyer's time cheap, by handing them three specific questions instead of fourteen pages.

## What is missing gets reported first

Absence is invisible when you read a document straight through. You notice bad clauses; you do not notice the one that is not there.

So the review starts with what should exist and does not: payment terms, what happens when scope changes, termination by either side, what happens to work in progress, who owns the output, liability caps, and anything agreed verbally that never made it in.

## Three terms that catch small businesses most often

| Term | Why it catches people |
|---|---|
| **Auto-renewal** | The window to stop it closes silently, usually months before renewal. Nobody diarises it |
| **Uncapped liability** | Disproportionate on a small contract, and common enough to look normal |
| **Payment on "acceptance"** | With no definition of acceptance, the term is open-ended and you cannot chase it |

Each is found, stated in plain terms, and costed against your cash where it affects it.

## Unusual is not the same as unfavourable

Conflating these wastes a negotiation, and it is the thing that makes an owner look unfamiliar with the market.

**Unusual** means not typical for this kind of agreement. Worth questioning, and often removed simply by asking. **Unfavourable but standard** means normal for this deal type. Arguing it rarely moves and costs you credibility on the items that would have.

The review labels each, so the list you take back is one the other side recognises as reasonable.

## What good output looks like

A strong review is mostly short. Two or three items to raise, one or two for a lawyer, and everything else marked accept and note.

The "what is missing" section is usually where the value is, and it is usually uncomfortable, because the thing missing is often the thing you discussed and assumed was covered.

If it comes back saying it could not assess several terms, check whether the agreement references schedules, annexures or policies that were not attached. **Those are frequently where the real terms live**, and it will say so rather than reviewing around them.

## Using it

Paste the agreement and say which side you are on.

Examples that work:

- "Client MSA, we are the supplier, worth about 30 lakh a year. What should I raise?"
- "Distributor agreement up for renewal. What did we agree to last time that I should change?"
- "This is take it or leave it. Tell me what I am accepting."
- "Office lease, five years. What is unusual in here?"
- "I only have budget for an hour of legal time. What should I spend it on?"

That last one is the best use of it.

## Limits worth knowing

- **It is not legal advice and it is not a lawyer.** It explains commercial meaning and flags what to ask about.
- It does not say whether a clause is enforceable or valid, or what your rights are. Those depend on jurisdiction and law.
- It does not draft contract language. It says what you want a term to achieve, and a lawyer writes it.
- **It cannot see documents referenced but not attached**: schedules, annexures, policies incorporated by reference. Those are frequently where the real terms are.
- It does not tell you whether to sign. It tells you what you would be signing.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It will not say if a clause is enforceable | Deliberate, and not negotiable | That is a legal question. It gives you the commercial meaning instead. |
| Long "could not assess" section | Schedules or annexures not attached | Attach them. That is often where the real terms sit. |
| Fewer findings than you expected | Most terms genuinely were standard | Check the "unusual" section. A short list is a good outcome. |
| It flags something as standard that you dislike | Unfavourable but normal for this deal type | Arguing it rarely moves. Spend the leverage on the unusual items. |
| No draft wording provided | It does not write contract language | It says what the term should achieve. A lawyer writes it. |
| Missing terms you thought were covered | They were discussed and never written in | That is the finding, and it is the commonest one. |
