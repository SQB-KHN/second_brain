# Contract and Commercial Terms Review

The contract arrives as a PDF, it is fourteen pages, and the work starts on Monday. Reading it properly means knowing which clauses will matter and which are standard, which is the thing nobody teaches an owner. So it gets signed. The clause that mattered surfaces two years later, in a dispute, when all the leverage has gone.

## When to use this

Trigger on: contract review, agreement, MSA, terms and conditions, vendor agreement, client contract, lease, distributor agreement, NDA, purchase order terms, renewal, service agreement, "should I sign this", "what should I look out for".

## Before you start

Required: the agreement, or the terms in question.

Ask, briefly:
1. **Which side you are on**, and what you are getting or giving. The same clause is protective or dangerous depending on which end of it you sit.
2. What the deal is worth, and over what period. A clause worth arguing about on a fifty lakh contract is not worth the relationship on a fifty thousand one.
3. **What matters most to you:** getting paid, being able to leave, protecting something you own, or limiting what you could be liable for. Review effort goes there first.
4. Whether this is negotiable or take it or leave it. Both are common and they produce different outputs: a negotiation list, or a risk list.
5. Anything agreed verbally that should be in here. The commonest contract problem is not a bad clause but a missing one.

## The line this skill will not cross

**This is not legal advice and it is not a substitute for a lawyer.**

What it does is read the document carefully, explain in plain language what each significant term means for you commercially, flag what is unusual, and tell you which items are worth a lawyer's time and why. That is genuinely useful, and it is a different thing from advice.

Where a term's consequences depend on jurisdiction, statute or case law, it says so and stops. **It will not tell you whether a clause is enforceable, and it will not tell you what your rights are.**

## Workflow

### 1. Find what is missing before reading what is there

Absence is invisible when you read a document straight through, and it is the commonest problem. Check for:

- Payment terms, and what happens when payment is late
- Scope, and what happens when scope changes
- Termination by either side, with notice
- What happens to work in progress or money paid, on termination
- Who owns what is produced
- Confidentiality, in both directions
- Liability, and whether it is capped
- Dispute process, and where
- Anything agreed verbally that is not written here

### 2. Read the money terms first, because they are the ones that bite

- **When payment is due, and from what event:** invoice, delivery, acceptance, or something vaguer. "Net 30 from acceptance" with no definition of acceptance is an open-ended term.
- What triggers an invoice
- Late payment consequences, if any
- Retention, holdback or milestone conditions
- Who bears taxes, duties and bank charges
- Price escalation, and whether you can raise prices at all during the term
- Whether payment can be withheld, and on what grounds

For each, state it in plain terms and say what it means for your cash.

### 3. Then the exit terms, because they set your leverage for the whole relationship

- Can you leave, on what notice, and what does it cost
- Can they leave, and what happens to you
- **Auto-renewal, and the window to stop it.** This is the term that most often catches a small business, because the window closes silently.
- What survives termination
- Exclusivity or non-compete, its scope and its duration

### 4. Then the risk terms

- **Liability: capped, uncapped, or unstated.** An uncapped liability on a small contract is a disproportionate risk and is worth naming even where it is standard.
- Indemnity: what you are promising to cover, and whether it is mutual
- Insurance required, and whether you actually have it
- Penalties, service levels, and what triggers them
- Force majeure, and whether it covers what actually happens

### 5. Then ownership and confidentiality

Who owns what is created and whether you can reuse it. Whether their confidentiality obligation matches yours, since one-way agreements are common and often unnecessary. Data, and who is responsible for it.

### 6. Separate unusual from unfavourable but standard

Two different things, and conflating them wastes a negotiation:

- **Unusual.** Not typical of this kind of agreement. Worth questioning, and often removed simply by asking.
- **Unfavourable but standard.** Normal for this kind of deal. Arguing it makes you look unfamiliar with the market and it rarely moves.

Saying which each is what makes a negotiation list credible.

### 7. Rank what to raise, realistically

- Must change before signing
- Worth asking for, likely to be given
- Worth asking for, likely to be refused, and whether to spend leverage on it
- Accept and note

Then state plainly which items are for a lawyer rather than a negotiation.

## Output format

**1. Verdict.** What is worth raising before signing, in two or three lines.

**2. What is missing.** Terms you would expect and cannot find.

**3. Money terms.** Each in plain language, with what it means for your cash.

**4. Exit terms.** Notice, cost, auto-renewal window, what survives.

**5. Risk terms.** Liability, indemnity, penalties, insurance.

**6. Ownership and confidentiality.**

**7. Unusual against standard.** Clearly separated.

**8. Ranked list to raise.** Must change, worth asking, unlikely, accept.

**9. For a lawyer.** The specific items and why, so the time is spent well.

**10. What this review could not assess.**

## Quality bar

- Is what is missing reported before what is present
- Is every significant term restated in plain language rather than quoted back
- Does every money term state what it means for cash
- Is the auto-renewal window found and stated, where one exists
- Is unusual clearly separated from unfavourable but standard
- Does the ranked list distinguish what will be given from what will be refused
- Are the items for a lawyer specific, rather than a general recommendation to get one
- Does it avoid saying whether any clause is enforceable
- Would an owner with no legal background know exactly what to do next

## What this skill does not do

- **It is not legal advice and it is not a lawyer.** It explains commercial meaning and flags what to ask about.
- It does not say whether a clause is enforceable or valid, or what your rights are. Those depend on jurisdiction and law.
- It does not draft contract language for you to insert. It says what you want a term to achieve, and a lawyer writes it.
- It cannot see documents referenced but not attached: schedules, annexures, policies incorporated by reference. Those are frequently where the real terms are, and it will say so.
- It does not tell you whether to sign. It tells you what you would be signing.
