# Difficult Commercial Conversations

Structures the four conversations owners avoid, a price rise, an overdue payment, refusing scope or a discount, and ending a relationship, with all three likely responses answered before you send anything.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who has to have the conversation themselves: SME owners and founders, agency and consultancy owners, freelancers and solo consultants, professional practice owners, distributors and suppliers, and anybody who is both the person doing the work and the person chasing the payment for it.

## The problem it solves

Four conversations decide more of a small business's profit than anything else, and owners avoid all four.

Raising a price. Chasing money that is owed. Refusing work that has crept past what was agreed. Ending a relationship that costs more than it returns.

Each gets deferred until it is worse. The price rise that would have been routine in March becomes a large correction in November, and the invoice that was thirty days overdue becomes ninety. By then the conversation is emotional rather than commercial, which is exactly when it goes badly. The other failure is subtler: the message gets written while the decision is still being made, so it sounds open when it was meant to be final, producing an apology where a statement was needed and an opening the other side takes immediately.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** what has happened, and what you want to be true afterwards.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What you have decided, and what is still negotiable** | A message that sounds open when the decision is made invites a negotiation you did not intend |
| The relationship: length, value, whether you want it to continue | A customer you want to keep needs a different document from one you can lose |
| **What has already been said, and by whom** | For overdue payment especially, the history decides whether this is a reminder or an escalation |
| What you would accept if they push back, and whether a contract covers this | Decide it now rather than while writing, and know what you can and cannot say |

## What you get back

1. **What you decided**, in one line, before any message
2. The message, in your register, ready to send
3. **A channel recommendation**, including where this should not be a message at all
4. **The three likely responses**, each with your pre-decided answer
5. What you would want in return for an exception
6. What is not changing, stated inside the message
7. For payment: which stage this is, and what the next one would be
8. What not to say, and why

## Decide before you write

Most of these go badly because the decision was still forming while the message was being drafted.

So the first output is not the message. It is one line stating what you decided; then the message states it, gives the reason once, and stops. **Lead with the decision, not the justification.** The instinct is to explain first, because the explanation feels like it earns the right to the decision. It reads as a negotiating position, and it invites a reply to the reasoning rather than to the decision.

## Four rules that do most of the work

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **One reason, not three** | Three read as weaker than one, because defeating one of three feels like defeating the message |
| **No apology for a commercial decision** | Softening is fine. Apology says the decision is wrong and invites you to reverse it |
| **Say what is not changing** | The most effective de-escalating sentence available. It turns a threat into a change |
| **Write it so it can be forwarded** | These get shown to a boss, a partner or a lawyer. That test removes sarcasm and anything you would not want quoted |

## The three responses, decided in advance

Every one of these conversations gets one of three replies:

- **They accept.** What happens next, and does anything need confirming in writing.
- **They push back and ask for an exception.** What is your answer, and what do you want in return? **An exception given for nothing becomes the new price**, for them and for whoever they tell.
- **They threaten to leave, or go silent.** What is your position, and is that outcome acceptable? If it is not, that changes the message before you send it, not after.

Answering all three in advance is the whole exercise, and it is why the skill asks what you would accept before writing anything.

## Some of these must not be an email

A skill that hands over a well-written email for a conversation that needed a phone call has done harm, so the channel is recommended deliberately:

- **Ending a long relationship should be a call.** An email is efficient and it is remembered as a discourtesy, and the industry is smaller than it looks.
- **Chasing a large amount** where the relationship matters is a call first, with the written record after.
- **A price rise to a large customer** is a call, and the letter follows and confirms it.
- **Refusing scope creep** works better in writing, because it needs a record.

## Payment escalates in stages

Each stage does one thing more than the last, and each is a separate message: a reminder that assumes an oversight, a firmer request naming the terms and the age, a statement of what happens next, and only then the step that changes the relationship.

**Do not skip stages, and never threaten anything you will not do.** An empty threat teaches that customer your terms are decorative, and it teaches everyone they talk to.

## What good output looks like

A strong message is shorter than you expected and states the decision in the first two lines. If the reason runs to a paragraph, the decision was not settled before writing.

It should also contain a sentence about what is not changing, which does more to keep a relationship intact than any amount of softening around the decision itself. And if it tells you to pick up the phone instead of sending what it just wrote, take that seriously. It only says so for the conversations where the written version is remembered as a discourtesy for years.

## Using it

Say what happened and what you have decided.

Examples that work:

- "Raising prices 9 percent from April. Twelve customers, one of them is a third of my revenue."
- "Invoice is 74 days overdue. I have sent two polite reminders. What now?"
- "Client keeps adding to the scope. I need to say no without losing them."
- "They asked for 15 percent off. What do I say, and what should I ask for in return?"

Telling it what you would accept before it writes is what stops the message negotiating against you.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- **It does not make the decision.** It structures one you have already made, and it will ask you to make it first.
- It does not send anything, and it does not negotiate on your behalf.
- **It does not draft legal notices, demand letters, or anything intended to have legal effect.** Those go to a lawyer, and it will say when you have reached that point.
- It does not tell you what a customer will do. It tells you what to have ready for each of the three things they might.
- It does not manufacture a reason. If the real reason is that the price was too low, that is the reason.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It asks what you decided first | The decision was not settled | That is the point. A half-made decision produces a message that negotiates. |
| Message shorter than you expected | One reason, no apology, no throat-clearing | Length reads as uncertainty in these four conversations. |
| It says to call instead of send | Ending a relationship, or a large sensitive amount | Take it seriously. The written version gets remembered. |
| It will not draft a legal notice | Deliberate, and not negotiable | It tells you when you have reached the point of needing a lawyer. |
| It priced the exception | An exception given for nothing becomes the new price | Decide what you want in return before you offer anything. |
| Tone feels colder than yours | Written so it can be forwarded to a third party | Ask for your register. The structure stays. |
