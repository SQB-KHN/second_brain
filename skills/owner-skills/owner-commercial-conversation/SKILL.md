# Difficult Commercial Conversations

Four conversations decide more of a small business's profit than anything else, and owners avoid all four. Raising a price. Chasing money that is owed. Refusing work that has crept past what was agreed. Ending a relationship that costs more than it returns. Each gets deferred until it is worse, then handled at the point where it has become emotional rather than commercial.

## When to use this

Trigger on: price increase letter, payment reminder, chasing payment, overdue, scope creep, saying no to a customer, firing a customer, ending a supplier, renegotiating, difficult conversation, "how do I tell them", "they want a discount".

## Before you start

Required: what has happened, and what you want to be true afterwards.

Ask, briefly:
1. **What you have already decided, and what is still negotiable.** A message that sounds open when the decision is made invites a negotiation you did not intend to have.
2. The relationship: how long, how much it is worth, and whether you want it to continue. A conversation with a customer you want to keep is a different document from one with a customer you are willing to lose.
3. **What has already been said, and by whom.** Particularly for overdue payment, where the history decides whether this is a reminder or an escalation.
4. What you would accept if they push back. Decide it now, not while writing.
5. Whether a contract or written terms cover this, since it changes what you can say and what you should not.

## The rule that matters

**Decide before you write.**

Most of these go badly because the decision was still being made while the message was being written. That produces an apology where a statement was needed, a range where a number was needed, and an opening the other side will take.

Size the decision first. Then the message states it, gives the reason once, and stops.

## Workflow

### 1. Lead with the decision, not the justification

The instinct is to explain first and land the message at the end, because the explanation feels like it earns the right to the decision. It does not. It reads as a negotiating position, and it invites a response to the reasoning rather than to the decision.

State it, give the reason in one or two sentences, and say what happens next.

### 2. Give the reason once, and do not stack reasons

Three reasons read as weaker than one, because somebody who defeats one of three feels they have defeated the message.

The reason should be true and yours: costs have risen, this is outside what we agreed, the terms are thirty days and we are at ninety. **Do not invent a policy that does not exist, and do not blame a third party for your own decision.**

### 3. Do not apologise for a commercial decision

Softening is fine. Apology is not, because it says the decision is wrong and invites the other side to ask you to reverse it. There is a real difference between "I know the timing is not ideal" and "I am really sorry to have to do this".

### 4. Say what is not changing

The single most effective de-escalating sentence available here. What stays the same: the service, the terms, the relationship, the person they deal with.

It converts a message that reads as a threat into one that reads as a change.

### 5. Pre-decide all three responses

Every one of these gets one of three replies, and deciding your answer now is the whole exercise:

- **They accept.** What happens next, and does anything need confirming in writing.
- **They push back and ask for an exception.** What is your answer, and what would you want in return? **An exception given for nothing becomes the new price.**
- **They threaten to leave, or do not reply.** What is your position, and is that outcome acceptable? If it is not, that changes the message before you send it rather than after.

### 6. Choose the channel deliberately

Some of these must not be an email:

- **Ending a long relationship should be a call.** An email is efficient and it is remembered as a discourtesy, and the industry is smaller than it looks.
- **Chasing a large or sensitive amount** where the relationship matters is a call first, with the written record after.
- **A price rise to a large customer** is a call, and the letter follows and confirms.
- **Refusing scope creep** works better in writing, because it needs a record.

Say which this is and why. A skill that hands over a well-written email for a conversation that needed a phone call has done harm.

### 7. For overdue payment, escalate in defined stages

Each stage does one thing more than the last, and each is a separate message: a reminder that assumes an oversight, a firmer request naming the terms and the age, a statement of what happens next, and only then the step that changes the relationship.

**Do not skip stages and do not threaten anything you will not do.** An empty threat teaches that customer your terms are decorative, and it teaches everyone they talk to.

### 8. Write it so it can be forwarded

These messages get shown to somebody else: a boss, a partner, a lawyer. Write assuming a third party with no context will read it.

That single test removes sarcasm, exasperation, and anything you would not want quoted back.

## Output format

**1. What you decided**, in one line, before any message.

**2. The message.** In the register you asked for, ready to send.

**3. Channel recommendation, and why.** Including where this should not be a message at all.

**4. The three responses**, each with your pre-decided answer.

**5. What you would want in return** for an exception.

**6. What is not changing**, stated inside the message.

**7. For payment:** which stage this is, and what the next stage would be.

**8. What not to say**, and why.

## Quality bar

- Is the decision stated before the justification
- Is there one reason rather than a stack of them
- Is there no apology for a commercial decision
- Does the message say what is not changing
- Are all three likely responses answered in advance
- Is an exception priced, so it is never given for nothing
- Is the channel recommended deliberately, including where a message is the wrong medium
- For overdue payment, is the stage named, with nothing threatened that would not be done
- Could this be forwarded to a third party without embarrassment
- Was the sender's floor decided before the message was written

## What this skill does not do

- It does not make the decision. It structures one you have already made, and it will ask you to make it first.
- It does not send anything, and it does not negotiate on your behalf.
- **It does not draft legal notices, demand letters, or anything intended to have legal effect.** Those go to a lawyer, and it will say when you have reached that point.
- It does not tell you what a customer will do. It tells you what to have ready for each of the three things they might do.
- It does not manufacture a reason. If the real reason is that the price was too low, that is the reason.
