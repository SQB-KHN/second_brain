# Deal Qualification and Risk Review

A deal at 80 percent usually means the rep feels good about it. Feeling good is not a stage. The questions that decide whether a deal closes are boring and specific, and they get skipped because asking them risks hearing an answer nobody wants. This asks them, and separates what happened from what somebody believes.

## When to use this

Trigger on: deal review, qualify this deal, MEDDICC, MEDDPICC, BANT, SPICED, deal risk, opportunity review, deal strategy, "is this deal real", "what am I missing on this deal", "why do we keep losing at this stage".

## Before you start

Required: what you know about the deal. Notes, emails, call summaries, a CRM record, or just what you can tell it.

Ask, briefly:
1. What the deal is: what they would buy, roughly what value, by when, and what stage you have it in.
2. **What has actually happened, not what you believe.** Meetings held, who attended, what they said, what they asked for, what they sent you. Evidence is events. Everything else is inference.
3. Whether they use a formal framework. If they do, use theirs. If not, this covers the substance without the acronym.
4. What has gone quiet, and when. Silence is information, and it is usually the most recent information available.

## Check for personal data first

Deal notes name individuals. Say so once, note that roles matter more than names for this analysis, and offer to work with names replaced by roles. Then continue with whatever they choose.

## What this actually tests

Frameworks get argued about. The substance does not. Whatever the acronym, a deal is qualified when each of these can be answered with evidence rather than belief:

- What problem are they solving, and what is it costing them **in their numbers, not yours**
- Who has to approve this, and have you met them
- How do they buy: the process, the steps, the paperwork, who signs
- What happens if they do nothing, and can they live with that
- Who argues for you when you are not in the room, and how do you know
- Who else are they considering, including doing nothing and building it themselves
- What is the date driven by, on their side

The test for every one is the same: **what happened that makes you believe that?** An answer resting on inference is not an answer, it is a hypothesis, and it gets labelled as one.

## Workflow

### 1. Separate evidence from inference, line by line

Sort every statement the user supplied:

- **Evidence.** Something that happened. They sent the security questionnaire. The CFO joined the call. They asked for a reference.
- **Inference.** Something concluded. They seem keen. Budget is probably approved. The CFO is on board.

Put them in separate columns and keep them there. Most deal reviews go wrong because inference gets treated as evidence, and once it is written down nobody remembers which it was.

### 2. Rate every qualification area against what it rests on

For each area, record what is known, what it rests on, and what is missing. Then rate it:

- **Confirmed.** Evidence exists.
- **Claimed.** The customer said it and nothing has tested it.
- **Assumed.** The rep believes it and nothing supports it.
- **Unknown.** Nobody has asked.

Do not soften "assumed" into "claimed". The entire value of the exercise sits in that distinction, and it is the one a rep under quota pressure blurs first.

### 3. Quantify the problem in their numbers, or record that it is not quantified

A problem the customer has not quantified is not a business case, and it will not survive procurement or a finance review. If the number is yours rather than theirs, say so explicitly. If there is no number at all, that is the single most important gap in most deals and it belongs at the top of the risk list.

### 4. Map the decision, not the org chart

- Who approves, at what value threshold
- Who can say no on their own
- **Who is affected and has not been consulted**, which is where late-stage surprises come from
- What paperwork exists: legal review, security review, procurement, vendor onboarding, purchase order. Each has a duration, and the sum of those durations is the real close date.

State the earliest defensible close date **from the paper process alone**. Where that date is later than the forecast date, that is a finding, and it needs saying before the forecast is submitted rather than after it misses.

### 5. Test the champion instead of assuming one

A champion is not somebody who likes you. A champion is somebody who has spent their own credibility on you.

Ask what they have actually done: got you a meeting, sent an internal note, pushed back on a rival, given you information they were not strictly supposed to. If the answer is none of those, you have a friendly contact, and the deal is single-threaded whatever the org chart says.

### 6. Count the threads

How many people at the account have you spoken to, and how many would notice if your champion left tomorrow. Single-threaded deals fail for reasons that have nothing to do with your product, and it is the most common and most fixable risk in the whole set.

### 7. Name the competition honestly, including the two nobody counts

Doing nothing, and building it internally. Both win more deals than any named competitor and neither appears in most CRM competitor fields.

Ask what they are comparing on, and **whether those criteria came from you or from a rival.** Criteria you did not set are a rival's fingerprints.

### 8. Rank the risks, one next action each

Every risk gets: what it is, what evidence would resolve it, who to ask, and by when. A risk without a specific next action is an observation.

Then say plainly whether the stage and close date are supported. If they are not, name the stage and the date the evidence does support. That is the output the review actually needs and the one nobody volunteers.

## Output format

**1. Verdict.** Whether the stage and close date are supported, and the top two risks. Two lines.

**2. Evidence and inference.** Two columns, kept apart.

**3. Qualification table.**

| Area | What is known | What it rests on | Rating | What is missing |

**4. The problem in their numbers.** Quantified, unquantified, or quantified only by us.

**5. Decision map.** Approvers, blockers, the unconsulted, and the paper process with durations.

**6. Earliest defensible close date.** From the paper process, against the forecast date.

**7. Champion test.** What they have actually done.

**8. Threads.** Count, and what happens if the champion leaves.

**9. Competition.** Including doing nothing and building internally, and whose criteria are in play.

**10. Ranked risks.** Each with the evidence that would resolve it, who to ask, and by when.

**11. The stage and date the evidence supports.**

## Quality bar

- Is every statement sorted into evidence or inference, with none left ambiguous
- Does every qualification area carry a rating and what it rests on
- Is "assumed" used where the rep believes something and nothing supports it, rather than softened to "claimed"
- Is the problem quantified in the customer's own numbers, or explicitly recorded as unquantified
- Does the decision map include somebody affected who has not been consulted
- Is the earliest defensible close date computed from the paper process and compared against the forecast date
- Is the champion tested on what they have done rather than how they seem
- Does the competition section include doing nothing and building internally
- Does every risk carry a specific next action with an owner and a date
- Does it state the stage and close date the evidence actually supports

## What this skill does not do

- It does not decide whether you will win. It says what is known, what is assumed, and what is missing.
- It does not accept your stage or your close date. Where the evidence does not support them, it says so and gives the ones it does support.
- It does not write the email, the message or the pitch.
- It does not advise on discount, pricing or contract terms. Those go to whoever owns commercial approval.
- It cannot see anything you do not tell it. A review built on optimistic input produces an optimistic review, which is why it asks what happened rather than what you believe, every time.
