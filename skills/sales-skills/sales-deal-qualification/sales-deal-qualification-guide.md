# Deal Qualification and Risk Review

Tests one deal against what actually happened rather than what you believe, rates every qualification area, and names the stage and close date the evidence supports.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who has to defend a deal in a review: account executives and enterprise sellers, sales managers running deal reviews, inside sales reps, pre-sales and solution consultants, key account managers, and founders selling their own product.

## The problem it solves

A deal at 80 percent usually means the rep feels good about it. Feeling good is not a stage.

The questions that actually decide whether a deal closes are boring and specific. Has anyone met the person who signs. Has the customer put a number on the problem, or did you put one on it for them. What does their procurement process take, in weeks. Who argues for you when you are not in the room, and what have they actually done.

Those get skipped because asking them risks hearing an answer nobody wants. So the deal stays at 80 percent until the week it was meant to close, and then a security review appears that nobody had asked about. The fix is not a better framework. It is separating what happened from what somebody concluded, and refusing to let the second count as the first. That is mechanical work, which is why doing it outside your own optimism helps.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** what you know about the deal. Notes, emails, call summaries, a CRM record, or just what you can tell it.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What has happened, not what you believe** | Meetings held, who attended, what they said, what they sent. Evidence is events |
| The deal shape: what, roughly how much, by when, what stage | Gives it something to test the evidence against |
| Whether you use a framework | It uses yours. Without one it covers the substance without the acronym |
| **What has gone quiet, and when**, plus their paperwork steps | Silence is usually the most recent information available, and the earliest defensible close date comes from the paper process rather than your pipeline |

It names the individuals in your notes first, and offers to work with roles instead of names.

## What you get back

1. **A verdict**: whether the stage and close date are supported, and the top two risks
2. **Evidence and inference, in two columns, kept apart**
3. Qualification table: what is known, what it rests on, a rating, what is missing
4. The problem in their numbers, or an explicit record that it is not quantified
5. **Decision map**: approvers, blockers, the unconsulted, and the paper process with durations
6. **The earliest defensible close date**, from the paper process, against your forecast date
7. Champion test: what they have actually done
8. Threads: how many, and what happens if your champion leaves
9. Competition, including doing nothing and building internally
10. Ranked risks, each with a next action, an owner and a date
11. **The stage and close date the evidence actually supports**

## The four ratings, and why the third one matters

Every qualification area gets one of these, and the distinction between the middle two is the whole exercise:

| Rating | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed** | Evidence exists. Something happened |
| **Claimed** | The customer said it and nothing has tested it |
| **Assumed** | You believe it and nothing supports it |
| **Unknown** | Nobody has asked |

Most deal reviews collapse "assumed" into "claimed", because it feels close enough and sounds better out loud. It is not close enough. "Budget is approved" as something the customer said is a claim worth testing; the same words inferred from their enthusiasm is a guess, and it will be treated as fact by everybody downstream of you. It will not soften that, which is occasionally uncomfortable and entirely the point.

## Two dates, and the one that is real

Your forecast date is when you would like it to close. The earliest defensible date is when their paperwork can physically complete.

Legal review, security review, procurement, vendor onboarding, purchase order. Each has a duration. Add them up and compare against your date. Where the paper process date is later than the forecast date, that is a finding, and it needs saying before the forecast is submitted rather than after it misses.

This single calculation moves more close dates than any other part of a deal review.

## The champion test

A champion is not somebody who likes you. A champion is somebody who has spent their own credibility on you.

So the test is what they have done, not how they seem: got you a meeting you could not get yourself, sent an internal note, pushed back on a rival, given you information they were not strictly supposed to.

If the answer is none of those, you have a friendly contact. That is worth something, and it is not a champion, and the deal is single-threaded whatever the org chart looks like.

## The competitors nobody records

Doing nothing, and building it internally. Both win more deals than any named competitor, and neither appears in most CRM competitor fields.

It also asks whose criteria are in play. If the customer is evaluating on things you did not raise, those criteria came from somewhere, and that is usually a rival's fingerprints.

## What good output looks like

A strong review is uncomfortable in one specific place: it names a stage and a date lower and later than yours, with the reason. That is the output the review needs and the one nobody volunteers.

The evidence column should be shorter than you expected and the inference column longer. That is normal, and seeing them side by side is what changes how the next deal gets worked. If everything comes back confirmed, check whether you fed it beliefs written as facts: "they're keen" arrives as evidence unless you say what it rests on.

## Using it

Describe the deal and, more importantly, what has actually happened on it.

Examples that work:

- "Deal at 80 percent, closing this month. Here is everything that has happened. What am I missing?"
- "My manager thinks this one is soft. Tell me if she is right."
- "We keep losing at the same stage. Here are three deals. What do they have in common?"
- "Forecast call in an hour and this deal carries my number. Stress test it."

Giving it the meeting history rather than your assessment is what makes it useful.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not decide whether you will win. It says what is known, what is assumed, and what is missing.
- It does not accept your stage or close date. Where the evidence does not support them, it gives the ones it does.
- It does not write the email, the message or the pitch.
- It does not advise on discount, pricing or contract terms. Those go to whoever owns commercial approval.
- It cannot see anything you do not tell it. A review built on optimistic input produces an optimistic review.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Everything came back confirmed | Beliefs were supplied as facts | Say what each one rests on. "They're keen" is inference until you name the event. |
| It moved your close date out | The paper process does not fit your date | That is the finding. Better to know now than in week eleven. |
| Lots of "unknown" ratings | Nobody has asked those questions yet | Correct, and it is the agenda for the next call. |
| It calls your champion a contact | They have not spent credibility on you | Harsh and usually right. Test them with a specific ask. |
| Verdict feels pessimistic | It rates on evidence, not momentum | Add the evidence. If there is none to add, that is the answer. |
