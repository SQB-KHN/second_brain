# Forecast Commit Brief

Turns a deal list into the one page you submit with a number: three tiers with the deals behind each, what you are deliberately not counting, and what would have to be true to miss.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who submits a number and then has to defend it: sales managers and team leads, regional and area sales managers, account executives who forecast their own territory, sales operations, and founders reporting to a board or an investor.

## The problem it solves

A forecast gets submitted as a single number with no working behind it.

Then it gets defended monthly in a meeting where only two moves exist: hold the number, or lose credibility. Neither is a good option, and both are consequences of the same thing, which is that nobody can see what the number is made of.

The failure runs in both directions and it is the same failure. Happy ears and sandbagging are both undefended numbers, and the receiver cannot tell which one they are being handed, so they discount everything. A rep who consistently lands under commit and never says so is as unhelpful as one who consistently misses.

The document that prevents all of this is one page. It takes twenty minutes and it is almost never written, because the parts that make it credible are the parts nobody volunteers: what you are excluding, what would break it, and what your last two commits actually landed at.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the deal list with your own assessment of each, or the output of a pipeline review.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who receives it, and what they do with it | A number feeding a hiring plan carries a different obligation from one feeding a dashboard |
| The target and the period | Everything is stated against these |
| **What you committed last period, and what landed** | The most useful input available. Your track record is your credibility |
| Whether they want a number or a range | Either way the brief shows the range and names which figure is committed |
| **Anything known that has not reached the pipeline** | A verbal yes, a budget freeze, a reorganisation, a competitor win |

## What you get back

A one-page brief, roughly 350 to 450 words:

1. **The number**: commit, best case, upside, and which one is being committed
2. **What carries it**: the deals in commit, concentration stated, one line each on what is left, who owns it, by when
3. **What I am not counting**: deals excluded from commit, with the reason for each
4. **What would have to be true to miss**: two or three specific conditions, each with its value impact
5. **What I need**: each ask attached to a deal and a value, or "nothing"
6. **Track record**: committed against landed for the last two or three periods, and the pattern if there is one

Or the same content ordered for a verbal forecast call: the number, the concentration, the exclusions, the risks, the asks.

---

## Three tiers, built from evidence rather than stage

| Tier | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Commit** | You would stake your credibility on it, and the evidence supports the date. Named criteria, applied consistently |
| **Best case** | Would close if one specific named thing happens, stated per deal |
| **Upside** | Everything else, and labelled as not forecast |

Each tier shows its deals and their values, because a tier without its deal list is an assertion, and an assertion is exactly what the meeting will argue with.

It will not apply a stage probability and call the result a forecast. That is arithmetic performed on guesses, and it produces a number that looks calculated and is not.

## The section that buys the credibility

**What I am not counting.** Deals that look good and are not in commit, each with the reason: no economic buyer met, the paper process will not fit the period, slipped twice already, single-threaded.

This is the mechanism, and it only works in one direction. A forecaster who names what they are excluding gets trusted on what they include. You cannot claim that credibility without doing the exclusion, and most forecasts skip it because it looks like admitting weakness. It is the opposite.

## Conditions beat confidence percentages

"70 percent confident" invites a negotiation about the percentage. It tells the receiver nothing they can act on.

"If the security review on the largest deal takes more than three weeks it moves to next quarter, and commit drops by 40 lakh" tells them exactly what to watch, and lets them decide whether to help with it. That is a more useful sentence, and it is harder to write, which is why the brief asks for two or three of them.

## Your track record goes in the brief

Where you supply prior commits and actuals, they get stated: committed against landed, for the last two or three periods, with the pattern named if one exists.

A forecaster who consistently lands under commit should say so and adjust, and saying it is precisely what makes the next number believable. The instruction is not to hide it, and also not to over-correct it into a new form of sandbagging.

## What good output looks like

A strong brief passes one test: **the receiver can tell whether the number is sandbagged or optimistic.** If they cannot, the working is not visible enough.

It should also be uncomfortable to write in two places. The exclusions section names deals you were hoping to count. And if commit rests on one deal, that appears in the first paragraph rather than being averaged into a total, because a number that is really one deal should be presented as one deal.

If the "what I need" section says nothing, either it is a very clean quarter or you have stopped asking. Both are worth knowing.

## Using it

Give it the deals and your read on each.

Examples that work:

- "Forecast call Thursday. Here are my 22 open deals and where I think each one is."
- "Target is 1.8 crore. I can defend 1.1. Write the brief so it does not turn into an argument."
- "Last two quarters I committed 90 lakh and landed 71. Factor that in honestly."
- "My commit is basically one deal. How do I present that without pretending otherwise?"
- "Same content but for a verbal call. What order will my VP ask in?"

Supplying last period's commit against what landed is the input that most changes how the brief reads.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not produce the pipeline analysis. Give it the deal list and your assessment, or run the pipeline review first.
- It does not decide your commit. It shows what the evidence supports and says plainly when your number sits above it.
- It does not inflate a number to please a receiver, or deflate one to stay safe. Both are undefended numbers.
- It does not advise on discount or terms to pull a deal into the period.
- It does not commit anything on your behalf. The number is yours and it is your name on it.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Commit lower than you wanted | Evidence does not support the rest | It says which deals and why. Add the evidence or accept the number. |
| It insists on the exclusions section | Deliberate, and it is the point | That section is what makes the included deals believable. |
| Concentration in the first paragraph | Commit rests on one or two deals | Correct. A number that is one deal should be presented as one deal. |
| No track record section | Prior commits and actuals not supplied | Give the last two periods. It is the strongest credibility input available. |
| Runs past one page | Too much working included | The working belongs in the pipeline review. Ask it to cut to a page. |
