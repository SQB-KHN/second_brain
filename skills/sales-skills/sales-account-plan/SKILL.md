# Account Plan and Whitespace

Account plans get written once a year for a review, in a template, and never opened again. Meanwhile the account's revenue is concentrated in one product, one business unit and one relationship, and nobody has noticed because the total still looks fine. This finds the concentration before it finds you.

## When to use this

Trigger on: account plan, key account, account strategy, whitespace, expansion, upsell, cross-sell, land and expand, territory plan, distributor plan, QBR prep, account review, at-risk account, "how do we grow this account".

## Before you start

Required: what the account currently buys, and from which parts of their organisation.

Ask, briefly:
1. Purchase history by product and by business unit or location, with values and dates. This is what makes whitespace visible rather than theoretical.
2. **Who you know there, by role, and who knows you.** Relationship coverage is the most predictive input available and it is almost never written down anywhere.
3. What their business is trying to do this year, **in their words**, from their own communications rather than from your inference.
4. Contract or renewal dates, and any commitment or minimum in place.
5. Whether other suppliers hold parts of what you could serve, and which parts.

## Check for personal data first

Contacts are named individuals. Say so once, note that roles and coverage matter more than names here, and offer to work with roles instead. Then continue with whatever they choose.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish what they buy today, three ways

Break current revenue down three ways, because each one exposes a different risk:

- **By product or service.** Concentration in one line means a single product decision can end the account.
- **By business unit, location or region.** Concentration in one unit means you have one customer inside one customer.
- **By buyer.** Concentration in one buyer means one person leaving takes the revenue with them.

Compute every one of these in code, never in prose. Report each concentration as a computed percentage. Where any one exceeds a majority, say so plainly and name it as the account's primary risk, **ahead of any growth plan.** A growth plan layered on top of unmanaged concentration is the wrong priority order, and it is the order most account plans use.

### 2. Map whitespace as a grid, not a wish list

Rows are what you could sell. Columns are the units, locations or teams that could buy it. Every cell gets one of four states:

- Bought from us, with the value
- Bought from somebody else, with who where known
- Not bought by anyone, meaning the need may not exist
- **Unknown**

**The unknown cells are the finding.** Most account plans present every empty cell as opportunity, when the honest description of most of them is that nobody has asked. Say which is which, because they need completely different work: one is a sales motion, the other is research.

### 3. Assess relationship coverage against the buying centre

For each unit or decision that matters, list who you know and their role, then answer three things:

- Would this person take your call
- Have you met anybody who could say no
- **If your main contact left tomorrow, what would happen to this revenue?**

That last question is the point of the section. Report the number of single-threaded revenue lines and their total value, because it converts a vague worry into a number somebody has to look at.

### 4. Read their priorities from their words, not yours

Where the user supplies the account's own communications, an annual report, a public statement, a stated internal goal, use those words directly. Where they do not, mark the priority as inferred and say what would confirm it.

Then map which of your offerings connects to which of their stated priorities, and **which of your offerings connects to none of them.** That second list is where most expansion plans quietly fail, and it deserves saying out loud rather than being left off the page.

### 5. Size the plays and rank them by winnability, not by value

Each play gets: what you would sell, to which unit, connected to which of their priorities, who you would need to know, what has to be true, and roughly what it is worth.

Then rank on winnability against value. **The biggest number is rarely the next move.** The next move is usually the one where you already have the relationship and the evidence, and ranking on value alone is why account plans list five plays and deliver none.

Say plainly how many of these this team can actually run this quarter, given what they told you about capacity.

### 6. Put the risks before the growth

- Renewal dates inside the horizon, each with what has to happen before it
- Concentration risks from step 1
- Single-threaded revenue from step 3
- Competitors already inside the account, and what they hold
- Anything that has gone quiet, and for how long

This section goes **before** the growth plays in the output. An account with a renewal in nine weeks and one relationship does not need an expansion plan this quarter, and putting growth first is how that gets missed.

### 7. Set the review trigger

What would change this plan: a renewal date, a reorganisation, a champion leaving, a competitor win, a quarter with no contact. Name the trigger, because an account plan without one is an annual document, and this is meant to be a working one.

## Output format

**1. Headline.** Current revenue, the largest concentration risk, and the highest-winnability play. Two lines.

**2. Revenue today.** By product, by unit, by buyer, each with its concentration percentage.

**3. Risks first.** Renewals, concentration, single-threading, competitors inside, silence.

**4. Whitespace grid.** With unknown cells distinguished from genuine no-need cells.

**5. Relationship coverage.** Per unit, with single-threaded revenue counted and valued.

**6. Their priorities.** In their words where supplied, marked inferred where not.

**7. Ranked plays.** Winnability against value, each with what has to be true.

**8. Capacity check.** Plays possible this quarter against what the team has.

**9. Review triggers.**

Offer the Excel version with the whitespace grid as a working sheet. Build it only if asked.

## Checks to run before delivering

- Revenue by product, by unit and by buyer each sum to the account total
- Concentration percentages are computed, not described
- Every whitespace cell carries one of the four states, with none left blank
- Unknown cells are counted separately from no-need cells, and the difference is stated
- Single-threaded revenue is counted and valued
- Every play names which of the account's stated priorities it connects to, or is marked as connecting to none
- Priorities taken from the account's own words are distinguished from inferred ones
- Risks appear before growth plays in the output

## What this skill does not do

- It does not forecast what the account will buy. It sizes plays and ranks them on winnability.
- It does not tell you an empty whitespace cell is an opportunity. Where nobody has asked, it says nobody has asked.
- It does not advise on pricing, discount or contract terms.
- It does not research the account for you. It works on what you supply and marks everything inferred as inferred.
- It cannot see your CRM, their org chart or their budget.
