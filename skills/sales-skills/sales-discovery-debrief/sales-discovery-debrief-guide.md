# Discovery Plan and Call Debrief

Plans a discovery call as a sequenced question set with a stated objective, then debriefs it into what was learned, what was missed, and the agenda for the next call.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who sells by asking rather than presenting: account executives, inside sales reps and SDRs, sales managers coaching calls, pre-sales consultants, key account managers, counsellors and advisors in education, insurance and real estate, and founders doing their own discovery.

## The problem it solves

Discovery calls get prepared in the four minutes before they start, and never debriefed at all.

So the same questions get asked at every account regardless of what is already known. The answers live in one person's memory and reach the CRM as three lines. And what was **not** learned stays invisible, which is the expensive part, because it surfaces two months later as a security review nobody knew about or an approver nobody had met.

The other half of the problem is the call itself. Reps ask a good question, get a partial answer, and move on without noticing, which is the most common recoverable failure in discovery and invisible without going back over what happened. This does the prep and the debrief. The debrief half is the one almost nobody does, and where most of the value sits, because it turns a conversation into qualification data and produces the next call's agenda.

---

## What you give it

**For a plan:** who you are meeting, their role, and what you already know about the account.
**For a debrief:** your notes, or a transcript if you have one.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What you want to be true at the end of the call** | A call with no objective becomes a demo |
| Where the deal is, and what you already know | Stops the plan re-asking what is answered |
| **A transcript rather than notes** | Unlocks talk ratio, question count, monologue length, and questions left unanswered |
| Whether a manager is coaching or the rep is self-reviewing, and their role | Only the first gets coaching notes, and process questions land differently with an operator than an executive |

It names the individuals in your notes first and offers to work with roles instead.

## What you get back

**Plan mode:** an objective stated as a commitment you want, a sequenced question set with what each question establishes, the questions you are avoiding, your allowed two minutes of talking, and the exit ask with a fallback.

**Debrief mode:**

1. **What they said, quoted, kept separate from what you concluded**
2. Qualification picture, each area answered, partial or still unknown
3. **Still unknown, which is the next call's agenda**
4. Mechanics, where a transcript was supplied
5. **Doors opened and not walked through**
6. The next step, assessed strictly
7. Coaching notes, only if a manager asked

---

## Sequence does more work than the questions

Any competent seller can list good discovery questions. Almost nobody sequences them, and the order is where the value is.

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Current state before future state** | People describe aspirations fluently and reality badly, and reality is where the problem lives |
| **Open before closed** | A closed question early shuts things down, and you get answers to what you thought to ask rather than the situation as it is |
| **Problem, then impact, then ideal** | Skipping the impact step is why deals reach pricing with no business case |
| **Process questions late** | Asked early, "who approves this" sounds like qualification, which it is, and people close up |

Every planned question carries what it is trying to establish. A question whose purpose is not written down gets asked badly or skipped entirely.

## It writes down the questions you are avoiding

Every seller has two or three: is there budget, who else are you talking to, what happens if you do nothing, who else has to approve.

They get listed explicitly, because not asking does not make the answer better. It makes it later, and more expensive.

## The debrief keeps their words intact

Two columns, always. What they said, quoted or closely paraphrased. What you concluded from it, separately.

The quotes are the asset. They feed the deal review, the proposal and the account plan, and they stop being useful the moment they are paraphrased into your language. "The month-end close takes us eleven days and two people work the weekend" is usable everywhere. "They have inefficiencies in finance" is not.

## Mechanics, where you have a transcript

| Measure | What it catches |
|---|---|
| **Talk ratio** | In discovery the seller should be well under half. Most are not |
| **Question count, and how many were open** | A call with four questions was a presentation |
| **Longest uninterrupted stretch** | Past about ninety seconds from the seller in discovery is worth naming |
| **Questions asked and not answered** | The most common recoverable failure. A good question, a partial answer, and the seller moves on |
| **Where a pitch happened, and what triggered it** | Usually a moment of silence the seller filled |

## What good output looks like

In plan mode, a strong plan has an objective you could fail. "Agreement to a technical review with their IT lead inside two weeks" can be missed. "Understand their needs" cannot, which is why it gets written.

In debrief mode the still-unknown list is the test. If it is empty after a first call, either the call was extraordinary or the debrief was generous. The next step gets assessed strictly too: "they'll get back to me" is not one, and "let's circle back next quarter" is a polite no nobody has recognised. The doors-not-walked-through section is usually the surprise: a number mentioned in passing, a reorganisation, a frustrated aside about a colleague. Highest-value lines in any call, and they get lost because they were not the topic.

## Using it

Say whether you want a plan or a debrief, and give it what you have.

Examples that work:

- "Discovery call tomorrow with a head of operations at a 400-person manufacturer. Plan it."
- "Here are my notes from this morning. What did I miss?"
- "Transcript attached. What was my talk ratio and what did I not follow up?"
- "Second call with this account. What is still unknown from the first?"

Handing it a transcript rather than notes changes what it can tell you substantially.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not join, record or transcribe your call.
- It does not write the pitch or the deck. It plans the questions and reports what happened.
- It does not put words in the customer's mouth. What is not in your notes is not in the debrief.
- It does not assess the rep as a whole. Coaching notes attach to moments in one call, because one call is not a performance review.
- It does not decide the deal's stage. Hand the debrief to a deal review for that.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Plan feels long | Every question carries its purpose | Ask for the top six. The purposes are what make them askable well. |
| Still-unknown list is empty | Notes were thin, or the debrief was generous | Check against the qualification areas. A first call rarely answers all of them. |
| No mechanics reported | Notes supplied, not a transcript | Talk ratio and monologue length need a transcript. |
| It calls your next step weak | It has no date, person or purpose | That is the finding. Get a specific commitment before the call ends. |
| Coaching notes feel harsh | They tie to specific moments rather than generalities | That is what makes them usable. They are about the call, not the person. |
