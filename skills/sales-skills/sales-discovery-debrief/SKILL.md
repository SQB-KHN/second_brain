# Discovery Plan and Call Debrief

Discovery calls get prepared in the four minutes before they start, and never debriefed at all. So the same questions get asked at every account, the answers live in one person's memory, and what was not learned stays invisible until it kills the deal two months later. This does both halves, and the debrief half is the one nobody does.

## When to use this

Trigger on: discovery call, first call, call plan, prep for a call, call notes, call debrief, call review, qualification call, coaching, talk ratio, "what should I ask", "here are my call notes".

## Before you start

For a plan: who you are meeting, their role, and what you already know about the account.
For a debrief: the notes or the transcript.

Ask, briefly:
1. Which you need. A plan before the call and a debrief after it are different documents.
2. **For a plan, what you want to be true at the end of the call.** A call with no objective becomes a demo.
3. For a debrief, whether this is the rep reviewing their own call or a manager coaching. Only the second one gets coaching notes.
4. Where the deal is and what you already know, so a plan does not re-ask what is already answered.

## Check for personal data first

Notes and transcripts name individuals. Say so once, note that roles matter more than names here, and offer to work with names replaced by roles. Then continue with whatever they choose.

## Plan mode

### 1. Set one objective, as a commitment you want rather than information you want

"Understand their needs" is not an objective. "Agreement to a technical review with their IT lead inside two weeks" is. State the specific thing you want to leave the call holding.

### 2. Sequence the questions, because order does most of the work

The sequence matters more than the questions themselves:

- **Current state before future state.** What they do today, concretely, before anything about what they want. People describe aspirations fluently and reality badly, and reality is where the problem lives.
- **Open before closed.** Closed questions asked early shut the conversation down, and you get answers to the questions you thought to ask rather than the situation as it actually is.
- **Problem, then impact, then ideal.** What is happening, what it costs, what better looks like. Skipping the middle is why deals arrive at pricing with no business case behind them.
- **Process questions late.** Who approves, how they buy, what happens next. Asked early these sound like qualification, which is what they are, and people close up.

State what each question is trying to establish. A question whose purpose is not written down gets asked badly or skipped.

### 3. Write down the questions being avoided

Every seller has two or three questions they do not ask because the answer might be bad: is there budget, who else are you talking to, what happens if you do nothing, who else has to approve this.

List them explicitly. Not asking does not make the answer better. It makes it later, and more expensive.

### 4. Plan the two minutes of talking you are allowed

Prepare the short version of why you are worth the meeting, and nothing beyond it. A discovery call where the seller talks is not discovery. Do not prepare a pitch, because a prepared pitch will get delivered.

### 5. Name the exit

What you will ask for at the end, and the fallback if they will not give it. A call that ends with "I'll send you some information" has no next step, and that deal is already drifting.

## Debrief mode

### 1. Extract what was said, and keep it apart from what it means

Two columns. What they said, quoted or closely paraphrased, and what you concluded from it. Never merge them.

The quotes are the asset. They feed the qualification review, the proposal and the account plan, and they are worthless the moment they have been paraphrased into your language.

### 2. Fill in the qualification picture and mark the gaps

Against the same areas a deal review uses: the problem, its cost in their numbers, the decision process, approvers, champion, competition, timing driver. Mark each as answered, partially answered, or still unknown after this call.

**The still-unknown list is the most valuable thing here.** It is the agenda for the next call, and it is the output that never gets written down.

### 3. Report the mechanics where a transcript exists

- **Talk ratio.** Who spoke and roughly how much. In discovery the seller should be well under half.
- **Question count**, and how many were open
- **The longest uninterrupted stretch**, and by whom. Anything past about ninety seconds from the seller in discovery is worth naming.
- **Questions asked and not answered.** A good question, a partial answer, and the seller moves on. This is the most common recoverable failure in a discovery call.
- Where a pitch happened, and what triggered it

### 4. Find the doors nobody walked through

Go through their statements for anything that opened something up and was not followed: a mention of another vendor, a deadline, a reorganisation, a frustrated aside about a colleague, a number said in passing.

These are the highest-value lines in any call, and they get lost precisely because they were not the topic.

### 5. Assess the next step strictly

A next step needs a date, a named person and a purpose. "They'll get back to me" is not a next step. "Let's circle back next quarter" is a polite no that has not been recognised as one. Say which of these you have.

### 6. Coaching notes, only where a manager asked

Two things done well, two to change, each tied to a specific moment in the call rather than to a general habit. Do not produce an assessment of the rep. One call is not a performance review.

## Output format

**Plan mode**

**1. Objective**, as the commitment you want. **2. Question sequence**, each with what it establishes. **3. The questions being avoided.** **4. Your two minutes.** **5. The exit ask, and the fallback.** **6. What you already know**, so it does not get re-asked.

**Debrief mode**

**1. What they said**, quoted, kept separate from what you concluded. **2. Qualification picture**, each area answered, partial or unknown. **3. Still unknown**, which is the next call's agenda. **4. Mechanics**, where a transcript was supplied. **5. Doors opened and not walked through.** **6. Next step**, assessed strictly. **7. Coaching notes**, only if a manager asked.

## Quality bar

- Is the objective a commitment you want rather than information you want
- Does every planned question state what it is trying to establish
- Are current-state questions sequenced before future-state ones
- Does the plan name the questions the seller is avoiding
- In a debrief, are their words quoted and kept separate from your conclusions
- Is there a still-unknown list, specific enough to be the next call's agenda
- Are questions that were asked and left unanswered identified
- Is the next step assessed against having a date, a person and a purpose
- Does it say plainly when "they'll get back to me" is not a next step
- Are coaching notes tied to specific moments rather than to general habits

## What this skill does not do

- It does not join, record or transcribe your call.
- It does not write the pitch or the deck. It plans the questions and reports what happened.
- It does not put words in the customer's mouth. What is not in your notes is not in the debrief, and what you concluded is labelled as your conclusion.
- It does not assess the rep as a whole. Coaching notes attach to moments in one call.
- It does not decide the deal's stage. Hand the debrief to a deal review for that.
