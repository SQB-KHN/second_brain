# Progress Report and Parent Communication

Report comments get written for forty students in one evening, so they come out generic. A generic comment tells a parent nothing while still costing a teacher four hours. Meanwhile the comment that would actually change something at home, specific and with a job attached, is exactly the same length.

## When to use this

Trigger on: report card, report comments, progress report, parent meeting, PTM, parent email, term report, concern letter, progress summary, cohort report, training report, "write comments for my class".

## Before you start

Required: the evidence. Marks, observations, submitted work, attendance, whatever the comment has to rest on.

Ask, briefly:
1. **Who reads it:** a parent, the student, a form tutor, an academic head, or a training sponsor. Each needs a different document, and the second changes the register completely.
2. The format and any length limit, since most report systems cap comments and a truncated comment is worse than a short one.
3. **What has already been communicated.** A serious concern should never reach a parent for the first time in a report card.
4. The institution's conventions: what may be said, what may not, whether marks are shared, whether comparison is permitted.
5. What you want to happen next, if anything. A comment with no intended action is a description.

## Check for student data first

Reports are about named individuals, and that is appropriate. Say so once.

Note that where a whole class is being drafted in one pass, it can be done with **roll numbers or initials and the names reinstated by the teacher**, which is worth doing. Note also that everything produced here is a draft for the teacher, not something to be sent. Then continue with whatever they choose.

## The rule this skill enforces

**Describe the work and what to do next. Never the child.**

"Has not yet shown written working on multi-step problems, which is where most of the lost marks are" is a report comment. "Is careless and lacks focus" is a character judgement about a child. It will be read that way, remembered for years, and it gives a parent nothing to act on.

The same applies to potential. Nothing here says a student is bright, weak, gifted, a slow learner, or likely to struggle next year. A report records work in a period. It should not become a permanent characterisation.

## Workflow

### 1. Anchor every statement to evidence

Each comment traces to something specific: a mark, a piece of work, a pattern across submissions, an observation with a date.

Where a teacher's impression is genuine but unevidenced, either find the evidence or leave it out. **An unevidenced comment is the one a parent challenges, and the one the teacher cannot defend in the meeting.**

### 2. Write three parts, in this order

- **What the student can now do**, specifically. Not praise, a capability.
- **What is not yet secure**, described as the work rather than the child, with its evidence.
- **What would move it forward**, as one or two actions, and **say who does them.** A parent asked to "encourage more effort" has been given nothing. A parent asked to check that written working appears on maths homework has been given a job.

Balance is not a quota. A student doing well gets a comment saying specifically what they can do and what the next stretch is, not an invented weakness for symmetry.

### 3. Make it specific enough that it could not be another student's

The test is simple and it catches almost every generic comment: **swap the name and see whether it still reads true.** If it does, it is not a comment about this student.

Where forty comments are being drafted in one sitting, this is the check that matters most, because forty comments written in one evening drift toward a template without anybody deciding to.

### 4. Write for the reader you were told about

- **To a parent:** plain language. No education jargon, no acronyms, no unexplained assessment terminology. "Formative", "scaffolding" and grade band codes mean nothing outside a staffroom.
- **To the student:** second person, about the work, one or two actions, nothing they cannot act on themselves.
- **To an academic head or sponsor:** the cohort first, patterns and provision rather than individuals, and what is being asked for.

Where another language is needed, produce a draft and say it should be checked by a speaker before it goes out.

### 5. Handle a difficult message properly

A concern needs, in this order: what was observed, over what period, what has already been tried, what is being asked for, and what happens next.

**Never let a report card be the first notification of something serious.** If it would be, say so and recommend the conversation happens first. That is a professional judgement worth surfacing rather than writing around.

Do not attribute cause. A pattern in the work is observable. The reason behind it usually sits outside what a teacher can see, and guessing at it in writing is where reports do real damage.

### 6. Never compare, rank or predict

No comparison to classmates, no position in class, no predicted grade, no statement about next year. Compare the student against the standard and against their own earlier work, and nothing else.

Where the institution requires a grade or band, report it as the institution defines it and do not editorialise around it.

### 7. For a cohort report, lead with provision

For an academic head, a coordinator or a training sponsor: what the group can now do, where the gap is, what caused it where that is known, what is being done, and what is needed. Individual names appear only where a decision requires them.

## Output format

**1. Per student.** What they can now do, what is not yet secure with its evidence, and one or two actions with an owner.

**2. Draft comments.** At the required length, in the register for the stated reader.

**3. Specificity check.** Any comment that would still read true for another student, flagged for rework.

**4. Evidence map.** Each statement against the mark, work or observation behind it.

**5. Concerns needing a conversation first.** Flagged, with the recommendation that a report is not the first notification.

**6. Unevidenced impressions.** Listed separately, for the teacher to evidence or drop.

**7. Cohort summary**, where asked for. Provision first, individuals only where a decision needs them.

**8. Translation note**, where another language was produced.

## Quality bar

- Does every statement trace to a mark, a piece of work or a dated observation
- Is every comment about the work rather than about the child
- Would each comment fail the swap test, meaning it could not be true of another student
- Does every comment name one or two actions, with somebody who does them
- Is there no comparison to other students, no rank, and no predicted grade
- Is the language free of education jargon where a parent is the reader
- Is anything serious flagged as needing a conversation before it appears in a report
- Are unevidenced impressions separated out rather than written in
- Does a strong student's comment say what they can do and what the next stretch is, rather than inventing a weakness
- Is it inside the length limit the reporting system allows

## What this skill does not do

- It does not describe a child's character, ability, effort, attitude or potential.
- It does not compare students, rank them, or predict a future grade or outcome.
- It does not attribute a cause to a pattern in the work. What sits behind it is usually outside what the evidence shows.
- It does not identify or rule out a learning difficulty, and it does not recommend an assessment. It describes what the work shows and names who is qualified to look.
- It does not send anything, and it does not produce a validated translation. Every comment is a draft for the teacher to check, edit and own.
