# Marking and Feedback Pack

Marks work strictly against your rubric, shows what the next level up would have required, and produces the cohort view of what the whole class got wrong.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who marks: school teachers in every subject, college and university faculty, coaching and test-prep faculty, tutors, teaching assistants, corporate trainers assessing submissions, and heads of department moderating other people's marking.

## The problem it solves

Marking sixty scripts takes a weekend, and the comments are the first thing sacrificed.

So students get a mark and a tick. They learn nothing from either, and they repeat the same mistake in the next assignment. The comment that would have changed something takes thirty seconds to write and never gets written, because there are fifty-nine scripts left and it is Sunday night.

There is a second loss, quieter and larger. Marking a set of scripts tells you what the *class* misunderstood, and that is the most valuable output of the whole exercise. But it only appears if somebody tallies the misconceptions across sixty papers, which nobody does at the end of a weekend of marking.

This applies your standard consistently, writes feedback that tells a student what to do next, and produces the cohort diagnosis before it produces a single individual script.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the student work, and the standard to mark against.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **A rubric, marking scheme or model answer** | Marking without a stated standard is marking against a mood, and it will not survive moderation or a parent. If you have none, it builds one and waits for your confirmation |
| **The task exactly as students received it** | "Explain" and "describe" earn marks differently, so a script that describes when asked to explain has a specific, nameable gap |
| Level and stage | A strong answer at class 8 and at class 12 are different answers |
| Whether a cohort view is wanted | The tally across scripts is where most of the value sits |
| Any accommodation in place | Changes how that work is read |

**It names the identifying fields first** and offers to work on de-identified scripts. Roll numbers or initials work throughout. Everything it produces is written for you, and is not addressed to a student until you decide it is.

## What you get back

1. **The cohort summary, before any individual script**: what the class got wrong, ranked, with counts
2. Per script, criterion by criterion: the level, the evidence in the work, and **what the next level up needed**
3. **Two next steps per student**, written as actions and ready to hand over
4. Wrong, missing and unclear separated, so technique is never confused with knowledge
5. Recurring misconceptions, counted, with the reteach note
6. **Rubric problems**: criteria that could not be applied cleanly
7. Needs the teacher: authorship questions, wellbeing concerns, out-of-range work. Described, never concluded
8. Marks, as a recommendation against your rubric for you to confirm

## The rule it will not bend

**It marks the work. Never the learner.**

"The conclusion restates the introduction instead of answering the question" is feedback. "You are careless" is a character judgement. It will be read as one, remembered as one, and it changes nothing about the next piece of work.

The same holds for ability. Nothing here says a student is weak, gifted, unable or likely to struggle. Where work suggests something you should look into, it describes the pattern and names who is qualified to look at it.

## Three things that get conflated, and the responses they need

| | What it is | The right response |
|---|---|---|
| **Wrong** | A misconception. They have an idea and it is incorrect | Name the misconception. That *is* the feedback |
| **Missing** | An omission. They knew it and did not write it, or ran out of time | Exam technique, not knowledge |
| **Unclear** | The marker cannot tell which of the two it is | Say so, and say what would settle it |

A misconception returned as "be more careful" survives intact to the next test. This separation is why it gets caught.

## Two next steps, not fifteen

The commonest failure in written feedback is volume. A script covered in comments is a script whose feedback will not be read.

So it picks the one or two changes that would move this work furthest and states them as actions. Everything else is a correction on the page, or nothing at all.

The test in the quality bar is the one that matters: **would a student who read only the next steps know exactly what to do differently?**

## What good output looks like

A strong pack leads with the cohort view, and that section is usually the surprise. Nineteen students making the same error is a teaching finding, not nineteen individual feedback problems, and it changes what you do on Monday rather than what you write on Sunday.

Every criterion should tell you what the next level needed. A student who knows they got 3 out of 5 has learned nothing; a student who knows what the fourth mark required can go and get it.

It should also flag at least one rubric problem on most sets. Rubrics are written before anybody has seen real work, and the cases they cannot cleanly judge only appear once sixty scripts exist.

## Installing it

1. Download this folder, or make a folder named `edu-marking-feedback` and put `SKILL.md` inside it. The guide does not need to go in.
2. Compress the folder to a `.zip`.
3. Go to Customize, then Skills, then add a skill and upload the zip.

Code execution is not required, though having it on lets it count misconceptions properly across a large set rather than estimating.

## Using it

Paste the work and the standard you are marking against.

Examples that work:

- "Six answers to this question, marking scheme attached. Mark them and tell me what the class got wrong."
- "I have no rubric for this assignment. Build one, then mark against it."
- "30 short answers. I only want the cohort view, not individual feedback."
- "Moderating a colleague's marking. Here are their marks and the scripts."
- "This student's answer is unusual. Is it wrong or is it missing something?"

Saying the exact command word from the question, and the marks available, changes the marking more than anything else you can add.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not award a final mark. It marks against your standard and hands it back for you to confirm.
- It does not describe the learner. No comment on ability, effort, attitude or potential appears anywhere.
- **It does not detect plagiarism or AI use, and will not give you a score for either.** Those tools are unreliable, and the cost of a false accusation against a child is not recoverable. It describes what is observable and refers it to you.
- It does not identify or rule out a learning difficulty.
- It cannot see handwriting, diagrams or working it was not given. A typed transcription may have lost the thing that mattered.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Marks stricter than yours | It applies the rubric literally where you apply judgement | That gap is useful. It usually means the rubric needs a clause. |
| It flags a rubric problem | The criterion genuinely cannot be applied to that case | Decide the rule once and it applies it across the set. |
| Only two comments per script | Deliberate | More comments means fewer read. Corrections still appear on the page. |
| It refuses to score AI use | Not negotiable | You get what is observable, described, for you to judge. |
| Feedback feels blunt | It addresses the work, not the person | Ask for it in the register you use with that class. |
| Cohort view thin on a small set | Too few scripts for a pattern | Counts are shown, so you can see how thin. Two of six is not nineteen of sixty. |
