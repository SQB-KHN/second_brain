# Marking and Feedback Pack

Marking sixty scripts takes a weekend, and the comments are the part sacrificed first. So students receive a mark and a tick, learn nothing from either, and repeat the same mistake in the next assignment. The comment that would have changed something takes thirty seconds to write and never gets written, because there are fifty-nine scripts left.

## When to use this

Trigger on: mark this, grade this, feedback, marking scheme, rubric, evaluate this answer, moderation, comments for students, "check my student's work", "what did the class get wrong".

## Before you start

Required: the student work, and the standard to mark against. A rubric, a marking scheme, a model answer, or the criteria stated in words.

Ask, briefly:
1. **The marking standard.** If there is none, build one first and get it confirmed. Marking without a stated standard is marking against a mood, and it will not survive a moderation meeting or a parent.
2. The task exactly as students received it, with the marks available and the command word. "Explain" and "describe" earn marks differently, and a script that describes when asked to explain has a specific, nameable gap.
3. Level and stage, so the standard is applied at the right one. A strong answer at class 8 and at class 12 are different answers.
4. Whether this is the whole set or a sample, and whether a cohort view is wanted.
5. Any accommodation in place for a student, which changes how their work is read.

## Check for student data first

Name which fields identify a student. Say once that **marking needs the work and an identifier, not a name**, and that roll numbers or initials work throughout. Offer to run on de-identified scripts.

Say also that this pack is written **for the teacher**, and is not addressed to a student until the teacher decides it is. Then continue with whatever they choose.

## The rule this skill exists to enforce

**Mark the work. Never the learner.**

Feedback describes what is on the page and what to do next. It does not describe the student. "The conclusion restates the introduction instead of answering the question" is feedback. "You are careless" is a character judgement, it will be read as one, remembered as one, and it changes nothing about the next piece of work.

The same applies to inference about ability. Nothing here says a student is weak, gifted, unable or likely to struggle. Where work suggests something a teacher should look into, describe the pattern and name who is qualified to look at it.

## Workflow

### 1. Apply the standard, not your taste

Mark strictly against the supplied rubric, criterion by criterion. Where the work is good in a way the rubric does not credit, say so separately rather than silently awarding for it.

Where the rubric is ambiguous on a case in front of you, **flag it as a rubric problem rather than deciding quietly.** That decision has to hold across sixty scripts, and only the teacher can make it stick.

### 2. Show where each mark came from

Every criterion gets the level awarded, the specific evidence in the work that put it there, and **what the next level up would have required.**

That last part is the whole point. A student who knows they got 3 out of 5 has learned nothing. A student who knows what the fourth mark needed can go and get it.

### 3. Separate what is wrong from what is missing

These get conflated constantly and need different responses:

- **Wrong.** A misconception. The student has an idea and it is incorrect. Naming the misconception is the feedback.
- **Missing.** An omission. They knew it and did not write it, or ran out of time. That is exam technique, not knowledge.
- **Unclear.** The marker cannot tell which. Say so, and say what would settle it.

A misconception returned as "be more careful" survives to the next test intact.

### 4. Write two next steps, not fifteen

The commonest failure in written feedback is volume. A script covered in comments is a script whose feedback will not be read.

Pick the one or two changes that would move this work furthest and state them as actions. Everything else is a correction on the page, or nothing at all. Address the work, name the specific action, and avoid the comment that only makes sense to somebody who already understands the answer.

### 5. Build the cohort view, which is the part nobody has time for

This is where the skill earns its place. Marking one script is a task. Marking a set and knowing what the class did wrong is a diagnosis.

- The misconceptions that recur, with a count
- The criterion the class lost most marks against
- **Whether the loss is concentrated in a few students or spread across the class**, because those need completely different responses
- Anything the whole class did well, which is worth knowing and almost never reported
- Whether the task or the rubric caused the problem, evidenced by everybody failing the same criterion

### 6. Flag what needs the teacher, and stop there

Send back rather than resolve: work that may not be the student's own, work suggesting distress or a safeguarding concern, work far outside the expected range in either direction, and any case where the rubric could not be applied cleanly.

On authorship, describe only what is observable, such as a register that shifts sharply mid-script or content beyond what was taught. **Do not score, rate or conclude.** An accusation of cheating against a child, resting on unreliable evidence, is not a recoverable harm, and no tool should be its source.

## Output format

**1. Cohort summary, first.** What the class got wrong, ranked, with counts, before any individual script.

**2. Per script.** Criterion, level awarded, the evidence in the work, and what the next level needed.

**3. The two next steps per student.** Written as actions, ready to hand over.

**4. Wrong, missing, unclear.** Per script, so technique is never confused with knowledge.

**5. Recurring misconceptions.** Counted, with the reteach note.

**6. Rubric problems.** Criteria that could not be applied cleanly, and where the standard was ambiguous.

**7. Needs the teacher.** Authorship questions, wellbeing concerns, out-of-range work. Described, not concluded.

**8. Marks.** Presented as a recommendation against the rubric, for the teacher to confirm.

## Quality bar

- Is every comment about the work rather than about the student
- Does every criterion show the evidence in the work that produced its level
- Does every criterion state what the next level up would have required
- Are wrong, missing and unclear separated, so a misconception is never returned as carelessness
- Are there at most two next steps per student, written as actions
- Is the cohort view produced before the individual scripts
- Are recurring misconceptions counted rather than described
- Is anything about authorship or wellbeing described and referred, never scored or concluded
- Are marks presented as a recommendation to confirm rather than as a decision
- Would a student who read only the next steps know exactly what to do differently

## What this skill does not do

- It does not award a final mark. It marks against the standard you supply and hands it back for you to confirm.
- It does not describe the learner. No comment on ability, effort, attitude or potential appears anywhere.
- It does not detect plagiarism or AI use and will not give you a score for either. Those tools are unreliable, and the cost of a false accusation against a child is not recoverable. It describes what is observable and refers it to you.
- It does not identify or rule out a learning difficulty.
- It cannot see handwriting, diagrams or working it was not given. A typed transcription is a transcription, and may have lost the thing that mattered.
