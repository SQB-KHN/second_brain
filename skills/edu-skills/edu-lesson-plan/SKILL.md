# Lesson and Unit Plan

A lesson plan is usually a list of activities with a time against each. What is missing is the thing the plan exists for: how anybody will know, before the bell, whether the class actually learned it. So the lesson runs, the activities happen on schedule, and the gap surfaces three weeks later in a test.

## When to use this

Trigger on: lesson plan, session plan, unit plan, teaching plan, scheme of work, pacing, lesson objectives, training session plan, module plan, "plan a class on", "how do I teach".

## Before you start

Required: the topic, the time available, and the level.

Ask, briefly:
1. **What students should be able to do at the end that they cannot do now**, stated as something observable. "Understand fractions" cannot be checked. "Compare two fractions with unlike denominators and justify which is larger" can.
2. What they already know, and the prerequisites. A lesson built on a prerequisite the class does not have will fail however well it is planned.
3. Class size, and the range within it. Thirty students across a four-year attainment spread is a different plan from twelve.
4. **What is actually available:** board, projector, textbooks, devices, lab, worksheets, or none of those. A plan requiring what the room does not have is not a plan.
5. Whether this is one lesson or a unit, and any fixed assessment date it has to reach.

## Check for student data first

Lesson plans do not usually carry it. Where class lists or individual needs arrive, say once that the plan needs **the profile of needs, not named students**, and that support can be planned without naming anybody.

## Workflow

### 1. Work backwards from the check, not forwards from the activities

The order is objective, then how you will check it, then the activities. Almost every weak lesson plan is built in the opposite direction, starting from an activity somebody liked.

Write the check first: the specific thing you will look at or ask, at a specific point in the lesson, that tells you whether to move on. Then design the activities that get the class to where they can pass it.

### 2. State the objective so it can be checked

An objective needs an observable verb, the content, and the condition. "Balance a chemical equation with up to four compounds, without a reference table" is checkable. "Learn about chemical equations" is a topic heading.

One or two objectives per lesson. A lesson with five objectives has none.

### 3. Name the misconceptions before planning the teaching

This is the step that separates a plan from a timetable, and it is the most experienced thing in any lesson plan.

Name what students commonly get wrong on this topic, and plan for it deliberately: the example that exposes it, the question that surfaces it, the non-example that makes the boundary visible.

**Where the class has already shown a misconception**, from a previous test or lesson, use theirs rather than a general one.

### 4. Sequence for cognitive load, not for coverage

- Retrieval of the prerequisite first, briefly, because a class that cannot do the prerequisite cannot do the lesson
- New content in small steps, each with a check before the next
- **Worked example before independent practice**, and worked fully rather than referred to
- Practice that starts constrained and opens out
- **Nothing new in the last five minutes**, which is where new content goes to be forgotten

State what to cut if the lesson runs late, **chosen in advance.** Every lesson runs late, and the decision made at the bell is always worse than the one made now.

### 5. Build checks that produce evidence from everybody

A question answered by one raised hand is evidence about one student. Plan checks that surface the whole class: everybody writes an answer, everybody shows a card, everybody attempts the same problem while you circulate.

**For each check, state what you will do if the class fails it.** A check with no branch is decoration, and the branch is the entire reason to check.

### 6. Differentiate by need, never by labelling

Plan support and extension as part of the lesson, not as an afterthought for whoever finishes early:

- What support looks like: the scaffold, the partially worked example, fewer steps, vocabulary given in advance
- What extension looks like: the same content at greater depth, not more of the same questions
- What the whole class gets regardless

Describe these **by the need they meet**, never by naming students or grouping them by ability.

### 7. Be honest about the time

Total the minutes including transitions, settling and packing up, then compare against the actual period length.

Most plans are written for a period longer than the one that exists. Say what will not fit, rather than producing a plan that ends mid-activity.

### 8. For a unit, sequence the lessons and place the assessment

Prerequisite order first, then the assessment placed **where there is still time to act on it.** An assessment in the last lesson of a unit measures the unit and changes nothing.

Name the spare capacity. A unit plan with no slack meets its first absence, festival or fire drill and stops being a plan.

## Output format

**1. Objectives.** One or two, observable and checkable.

**2. The check.** What you will look at, when, and the branch if the class fails it.

**3. Prerequisites**, and the retrieval that opens the lesson.

**4. Misconceptions to plan for**, each with the example or question that exposes it.

**5. Lesson sequence.** Timed, with what to cut if it runs late.

**6. Checks for understanding.** Placed, whole-class, each with its branch.

**7. Differentiation.** Support, extension, and what everybody gets, described by need.

**8. Materials**, against what is actually available.

**9. Time total**, against the real period length.

**10. For a unit:** lesson sequence, assessment placement, and the slack.

## Quality bar

- Is the objective observable, so somebody in the room could tell whether it was met
- Was the check designed before the activities
- Does every check produce evidence from the whole class rather than from a volunteer
- Does every check carry a stated branch for what to do if the class fails it
- Are the topic's common misconceptions named and planned for
- Is the prerequisite retrieved at the start rather than assumed
- Is there a worked example before independent practice
- Is nothing new introduced in the last five minutes
- Is what to cut decided in advance rather than left to the bell
- Does differentiation describe needs rather than grouping students by ability
- Does the timing total fit the actual period, transitions included

## What this skill does not do

- It does not know your syllabus, textbook or scheme of work. Supply them and it plans within them.
- It does not produce the worksheets, slides or materials. It specifies what each has to do.
- It does not group your students by ability, and it will not build a plan that does.
- It does not know your class. It plans for the profile you describe, and that profile is yours to give.
- It does not guarantee a lesson will work. It builds the checks that tell you whether it did, while there is still time to act.
