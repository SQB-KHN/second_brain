# Lesson and Unit Plan

Plans a lesson backwards from the check rather than forwards from the activities, names the misconceptions to expect, and decides in advance what gets cut when it runs late.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who plans teaching: school teachers across subjects, coaching and test-prep faculty, college lecturers, corporate trainers and L&D professionals, instructional designers, private tutors, and academic coordinators reviewing other people's plans.

## The problem it solves

A lesson plan is usually a list of activities with a time against each.

What is missing is the thing the plan exists for: how anybody will know, before the bell, whether the class actually learned it. So the lesson runs, the activities happen on schedule, everyone leaves satisfied, and the gap surfaces three weeks later in a test when there is no time left to fix it.

Two other things go wrong reliably. The plan is written for a period longer than the one that exists, so it ends mid-activity and the decision about what to drop gets made at the bell, badly. And differentiation is an afterthought for whoever finishes early, rather than something designed into the lesson.

This builds the check first, names what students will get wrong before you teach it, and forces the timing to be honest.

---

## What you give it

**Minimum:** the topic, the time available, and the level.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What students should be able to do at the end that they cannot do now** | Stated observably. "Understand fractions" cannot be checked; "compare two fractions with unlike denominators and justify which is larger" can |
| **The prerequisites, and what they already know** | A lesson built on a prerequisite the class lacks will fail however well it is planned |
| Class size and the range within it | Thirty students across a four-year attainment spread is a different plan from twelve |
| **What is actually available** | Board, projector, textbooks, devices, lab, worksheets, or none. A plan requiring what the room lacks is not a plan |
| Misconceptions this class has already shown | It uses theirs rather than a general list, which is far more useful |
| Whether it is one lesson or a unit | Units get sequencing, assessment placement and slack |

Lesson plans do not usually carry student data. Where class lists or individual needs arrive, it notes once that the plan needs **the profile of needs, not named students.**

## What you get back

1. Objectives, one or two, observable and checkable
2. **The check**: what you will look at, when, and the branch if the class fails it
3. Prerequisites, and the retrieval that opens the lesson
4. **Misconceptions to plan for**, each with the example or question that exposes it
5. Lesson sequence, timed, **with what to cut if it runs late**
6. Checks for understanding, placed, whole-class, each carrying its branch
7. Differentiation: support, extension, and what everybody gets, described by need
8. Materials, against what is actually available
9. Time total, against the real period length
10. For a unit: lesson sequence, assessment placement, and the slack

---

## Backwards from the check

The order is objective, then the check, then the activities. Almost every weak lesson plan is built the other way round, starting from an activity somebody liked and working out afterwards what it teaches.

So the check gets written first: the specific thing you will look at or ask, at a specific point, that tells you whether to move on. Only then do the activities get designed, to get the class to where they can pass it.

**Every check has to produce evidence from everybody.** A question answered by one raised hand is evidence about one student. Everybody writes an answer, everybody shows a card, everybody attempts the same problem while you circulate.

And every check carries **a stated branch**: what you do if the class fails it. A check with no branch is decoration, and the branch is the whole reason to check at all.

## Misconceptions get named before the teaching

This is the step that separates a plan from a timetable, and it is the most experienced-looking thing in any lesson plan.

For the topic, it names what students commonly get wrong, then plans for it deliberately: the example that exposes it, the question that surfaces it, the non-example that makes the boundary visible.

Where your class has already shown a misconception, in a previous test or lesson, tell it and it will use theirs. A misconception you have actually seen beats a general one every time.

## Two honesty checks built in

**What to cut, decided now.** Every lesson runs late. The decision made at the bell is always worse than the one made while planning, so the plan names in advance what goes if time runs short.

**The timing totals include transitions.** Settling, moving, packing up. Most plans are written for a period longer than the one that exists, and it will say what does not fit rather than producing a plan that ends mid-activity.

## What good output looks like

A strong plan has a check you could fail. If the check is "ask if they understood", it is not a check. If it is "everybody writes the balanced equation for question 3 on their slate and holds it up", you will know within ten seconds where the class is.

The differentiation section should describe needs, never students. Support is a scaffold, a partially worked example, fewer steps, vocabulary given in advance. Extension is the same content at greater depth, not more of the same questions. **It will not group your students by ability, and it will not build a plan that does.**

If the timing comes back saying a third of the plan will not fit, that is usually accurate and worth knowing on Sunday rather than at the bell on Monday.

## Using it

Say the topic, the time, the level, and what the room actually has.

Examples that work:

- "40 minutes, class 8, balancing chemical equations. They have done the symbols and formulae already."
- "This lesson never finishes. Here is the plan. What should I cut and where?"
- "Unit plan for five lessons on trigonometry, term test on the 20th."
- "Half the class got fraction comparison wrong last week. Plan the reteach lesson around their actual error."
- "Two-hour corporate training session on giving feedback. 14 participants, no projector."

Telling it what your class already got wrong turns a good plan into a targeted one.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not know your syllabus, textbook or scheme of work. Supply them and it plans within them.
- It does not produce the worksheets, slides or materials. It specifies what each has to do.
- It does not group your students by ability, and it will not accept a plan built on doing so.
- It does not know your class. It plans for the profile you describe, and that profile is yours to give.
- It does not guarantee a lesson will work. It builds the checks that tell you whether it did, while there is still time to act.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Plan is shorter than you expected | Timing includes transitions and settling | Usually accurate. Check what it says will not fit. |
| It rewrote your objective | The original was not observable | "Understand X" cannot be checked. It converts to something that can. |
| Misconceptions look generic | It used a general list | Tell it what your class actually got wrong. Theirs beats any general list. |
| It will not group by ability | Deliberate, and not negotiable | You get support and extension described by need, which is the usable version. |
| Fewer activities than you planned | Each check needs time to run and act on | A check you skip for time was not worth planning. |
| Unit plan has gaps in it | That is the slack, named deliberately | A unit with no slack stops being a plan at the first absence or fire drill. |
