# Question Paper and Blueprint

A question paper gets written the week it is needed, from memory, and its blueprint is reverse-engineered afterwards to match whatever got written. So the paper over-tests whatever the writer finds interesting, the marks do not line up with the teaching time spent, and two ambiguous questions are discovered simultaneously by two hundred students.

## When to use this

Trigger on: question paper, test paper, unit test, exam paper, blueprint, weightage, sample paper, marking scheme, periodic test, worksheet, "set a test on", "check this paper".

## Before you start

Required: the syllabus or topics to be tested, the total marks, and the duration.

Ask, briefly:
1. **The blueprint, if one is prescribed.** Board and institutional formats fix marks per section, question types and often the cognitive split. Where a format is prescribed, follow it exactly rather than proposing a better one.
2. The class or level, and the board or curriculum, since command words, expected length and mark conventions all differ.
3. **Teaching time actually spent per topic**, which is the honest basis for weightage and is almost never used. A topic taught for six periods and tested for two marks is a mismatch students notice immediately.
4. What the paper is for: practice, a unit test, a term exam or a diagnostic. It changes the difficulty spread.
5. Question types to include or avoid, and whether choice is permitted.

## Check for student data first

Papers do not usually carry it. If scripts, marks or class lists arrive with a paper for review, say so once and note that a review needs the paper and the blueprint, not the students.

## Two modes

**Build** a paper to a blueprint, or **review** an existing paper against one. The review discipline runs in both, because a paper that has not been checked will be checked by its candidates.

## Workflow

### 1. Build the blueprint before writing a single question

This is the step that gets skipped, and the reason papers come out lopsided. A grid: rows are topics, columns are cognitive levels, cells carry marks allocated.

Then check the margins:

- **Topic totals** against teaching time, or against prescribed weightage where it exists
- **Cognitive level totals.** A paper that is 80 percent recall tests memory and predicts nothing about understanding, and it is exactly what a paper written from memory produces.
- **Question type totals** against the prescribed format

Show the grid and get it confirmed before writing questions. Writing first and reverse-engineering the blueprint afterwards is the failure this step exists to prevent.

### 2. Write each question to a cell, not to a topic

The cognitive level is a design constraint, not a label applied afterwards:

- **Recall:** state, define, list, name
- **Understanding:** explain, describe, distinguish, why
- **Application:** calculate, apply, solve, predict for a situation not seen before
- **Higher order:** analyse, compare, justify, evaluate, design

The command word has to match the intended level. "Explain" on a question answerable by naming three things is a recall question wearing an understanding label, and the blueprint it fills is a fiction.

### 3. Apply the item quality rules to every question

Most bad questions fail one of these, and each is checkable:

- **One thing at a time.** A question testing two ideas cannot be part-marked fairly, so a student who has one and not the other gets an arbitrary score.
- **No ambiguity.** Read every question as a student who knows less than you would read it. If two readings are possible, one of them gets defended in the staff room afterwards.
- **No unintended cues.** Option length, grammatical agreement, "all of the above", and a correct option more detailed than the others all give the answer away.
- **Plausible distractors.** Every wrong option should be wrong for a reason a student might actually believe. Distractors nobody picks turn a four-option question into a two-option one.
- **Nothing outside the syllabus**, including in the stem and the context.
- **No dependency chains**, where getting part (a) wrong makes (b) and (c) unanswerable. A student should lose marks once for one error, not three times.
- **No negatives unless necessary**, and never a double negative.
- **Context fair to everyone.** A word problem set in a context only some students will recognise tests the context.

### 4. Allocate marks to match the work

Marks should reflect what a student has to produce, not habit. A three-mark question needs three creditable points or three steps. Where the mark and the work do not match, one of them is wrong.

State the expected number of creditable points per question, because that is what makes a marking scheme consistent across two markers.

### 5. Test the time

Estimate the time each question needs, sum it, and add reading and checking time. Compare against the duration.

Papers are routinely set that cannot be completed, and the result measures writing speed rather than learning. Where the estimate exceeds the duration, say so and say what to cut.

### 6. Write the marking scheme with the paper, not afterwards

For each question: the creditable points, marks per point, what earns partial credit, what does not, and **the alternative correct answers a real class will actually produce.**

A marking scheme written after the scripts arrive gets bent to fit them.

### 7. In review mode, report against the same standard

Fill in the blueprint grid as found, not as intended. Then every question against the item quality rules with the specific problem named, mark arithmetic, syllabus coverage and anything outside it, dependency chains, and time feasibility. Close with a verdict: ready, ready with fixes, or not ready, blockers first.

## Output format

**1. The blueprint grid.** Topics against cognitive levels, marks per cell, margins checked.

**2. The paper.** Formatted as students will receive it, with marks and instructions.

**3. Question map.**

| Q | Topic | Level | Marks | Expected creditable points | Estimated time |

**4. The marking scheme.** Creditable points, partial credit rules, alternative acceptable answers.

**5. Time analysis.** Per section and total, against the duration.

**6. Item quality notes.** Anything that needed a compromise, and why.

**7. In review mode:** verdict, blueprint compliance as found, per-question findings, mark arithmetic, coverage, timing.

## Quality bar

- Was the blueprint built and confirmed before any question was written
- Does every question map to one specific cell in the grid
- Does every command word match the cognitive level it is credited against
- Does every question test one thing at a time
- Would a student who knows less than the writer read every question the way the writer intended
- Is every multiple choice distractor wrong for a reason somebody might actually believe
- Are there no dependency chains that make one error cost marks three times
- Do the marks per question match the creditable points expected
- Does the estimated time fit the duration, with reading and checking included
- Is the marking scheme written with the paper, including the alternative answers a real class produces
- Where a format is prescribed, is it followed exactly rather than improved

## What this skill does not do

- It does not know your board's current syllabus, prescribed format or mark conventions. Supply them and it follows them. It will not assume them.
- It does not set the difficulty for you. It builds to the spread you ask for and says what that spread implies.
- It does not guarantee a paper is error free. It applies the item rules and flags what it could not resolve; a subject expert still reads it before it goes out.
- It does not produce diagrams, graphs or figures. It specifies what each has to show.
- It does not predict how a class will score on it.
