# Mistake Analysis

You review a test by looking at what you got wrong, feeling bad about it, and moving on. Nothing changes, so the same mistakes appear in the next test, and the pattern that would have been obvious across four tests stays invisible because nobody wrote it down. This is the habit that separates people who improve from people who simply practise a lot.

## When to use this

Trigger on: mistake log, error analysis, test review, mock test analysis, silly mistakes, careless errors, accuracy, "why do I keep getting this wrong", "my score is not improving", "I know the concept but".

## Before you start

Required: your wrong answers, and enough of each question to know what was asked. A list of question numbers is not enough.

Ask, briefly:
1. **For each mistake, what you were thinking when you did it.** This is the whole input and the one students skip. Whether you knew the method and slipped, picked the wrong method, or did not know it at all is the entire distinction, and only you have it.
2. Whether the test was timed and whether you finished. An untimed test tells you about knowledge; a timed one tells you about knowledge and speed, which are different problems.
3. Whether earlier test data exists, so patterns can be found rather than one test described.
4. Negative marking, since it changes what a wrong attempt cost against a skipped one.
5. **Which questions you got right but guessed.** Most students throw this data away, and it predicts the next mistake.

## Check for personal data first

A marksheet or test report carries a name, a roll number and often a rank. Say once that this analysis works on the questions and your reasoning rather than on your identity or your rank, and offer to work without them.

## Workflow

### 1. Classify every mistake into exactly one of four categories

This is the whole skill, and the classification is the part students get wrong.

- **Concept.** You did not know it, or knew it incorrectly. The fix is study.
- **Application.** You knew the concept and did not recognise that this question needed it, or could not get from concept to answer. The fix is practice on varied question forms, not re-reading the concept.
- **Execution.** You knew what to do and erred doing it: arithmetic, a sign, a unit, transcription, misreading the question. **The fix is a process change, not more study.**
- **Time.** You could have got it and did not have the minutes.

**"Silly mistake" is not a category.** It is what students call execution errors, and calling them silly is exactly why they never get fixed, because it implies they are random and unfixable when they are usually a repeatable process failure with a specific cause.

### 2. Push back on the student's own classification

Students over-classify as execution and under-classify as application. Calling something a silly mistake is more comfortable than admitting the method was not solid, and the consequence is that the same question type keeps failing.

The test: **if you had unlimited time and could redo it now, without looking anything up, would you get it right?** If not, it was not execution.

### 3. Find the pattern across mistakes, which one test cannot show

- **By topic.** Which topics produce errors regardless of type.
- **By error type.** The proportion of knowledge, application, execution and time. This proportion changes the whole study plan.
- **By position in the paper.** Errors clustered late point at time or fatigue, not knowledge.
- **By question type or difficulty.** Failing the easy ones and passing the hard ones is a specific, common finding, and it usually means reading speed or overconfidence.
- **By repetition.** The same error in three tests is the finding. One instance is noise, and it gets labelled as one.

### 4. Analyse execution errors specifically, because they are the cheapest marks

Group them: sign errors, unit errors, arithmetic, transcription between lines, misreading the question, answering the wrong part.

Each has a different process fix, and the fix is a habit rather than a topic: box the final answer and check the units, write the sign convention at the top of the page, read the question twice before starting, underline what is actually being asked.

**Recovering execution errors is the fastest score improvement available to most students, and it requires no new knowledge.**

### 5. Count what was right but guessed

A correct guess is a wrong answer that has not happened yet. Count them, treat them as gaps, and file them with the concept errors.

Where negative marking applies, compute what guessing has actually cost or gained across the tests. Most students believe they guess well, and the arithmetic usually disagrees.

### 6. Analyse what was skipped, not only what was wrong

- Skipped and would have got it: a time or confidence problem
- Skipped and would not have: a correct decision, worth confirming
- Attempted and lost marks where skipping would have been better, under negative marking

Strategy improvement lives here, and almost no student reviews it.

### 7. Produce three or four actions, ranked and small

Each with what it addresses and how you will know it worked in the next test. Rank by marks recoverable: execution fixes first because they are cheapest, then application on the highest-weight topics, then concept.

**State plainly what is not being fixed this cycle.** A list of eleven things to work on is a list nobody works on.

### 8. Set up the log so it accumulates

The value is in the pattern, and a pattern needs several tests. Give the format and the minimum it must capture: question, topic, error type, and what you were thinking at the time. Four columns.

## Output format

**1. Headline.** Marks lost by error type, and the single biggest recoverable group.

**2. Error breakdown.** Concept, application, execution, time. Marks and share of each.

**3. Reclassified.** Where your own label was changed, and why.

**4. Patterns.** By topic, by type, by position in the paper, and by repetition across tests.

**5. Execution errors**, grouped, with the process fix for each group.

**6. Guessed correct.** Counted as gaps, with the negative marking arithmetic.

**7. Skipped analysis.** Right calls, wrong calls, and what that cost.

**8. Next actions.** Three or four, ranked by marks recoverable, each with its check.

**9. What is not being fixed this cycle.**

**10. The log format**, for next time.

## Checks to run before delivering

- Every mistake is classified into exactly one of the four categories
- Marks lost by category sum to total marks lost
- Any reclassification of the student's own label is stated with its reason
- Patterns are claimed only where something appears more than once, and single instances are labelled as single instances
- Guessed-correct answers are counted as gaps rather than as successes
- Negative marking arithmetic is computed where it applies
- Actions are limited to three or four, and what is not being fixed is stated
- The word "silly" does not appear as a category anywhere in the output

## What this skill does not do

- It does not predict your next score or your rank.
- It cannot classify a mistake without knowing what you were thinking. A list of wrong question numbers produces a topic list, not an analysis.
- It does not teach the topics behind your concept errors. It tells you which ones they are.
- It does not accept "silly mistake" as an explanation, and it will ask the question that tests whether it was one.
- It does not judge you on a score. It works on the errors, and a rank is not an input it needs.
