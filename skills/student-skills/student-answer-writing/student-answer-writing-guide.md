# Answer Writing and Review

Builds an answer's structure from its command word and its marks, then reviews your own attempt and separates what you did not know from what you knew and failed to get credited for.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone writing answers for marks: class 9 to 12 students across boards, competitive exam aspirants sitting subjective papers, civil services candidates, undergraduates and postgraduates, and anyone who keeps being told their answers are "not to the point" without being told what that means.

## The problem it solves

Two students know the same amount and get different marks.

The difference is not knowledge. One wrote an answer an examiner could mark quickly, and the other wrote everything they knew in a single paragraph, correctly, and lost half the marks for it. Nobody teaches this, and it is worth more marks than most of the content students spend their time on.

The single largest version is the command word. An answer that *describes* when asked to *explain* contains correct material and scores poorly, and the student concludes they did not know the topic well enough. They knew it; they answered a different question. The second largest is length: a five-mark question wants five things, not a page, and writing beyond what the marks justify is an unrecognised cause of low totals, because the time came out of the questions you never reached.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the question, and either your attempt or a request for the structure.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **The command word, exactly as printed** | Explain, describe, compare, derive and justify earn marks in completely different ways |
| **Marks available and expected length** | A three-mark and a fifteen-mark answer on the same topic are different documents |
| The exam and level | Boards, universities and competitive exams reward different things |
| A marking scheme, if you have one | It marks against yours instead of a general standard |
| Time available per question | A technically better answer you cannot finish is worth less |

## What you get back

**Structure mode:** what the command word requires, the length and mark arithmetic, the structure in order with what earns marks in each part, what to cut, diagram and working requirements, and how long this should take.

**Review mode:**

1. Marks as they would be awarded, against your scheme where supplied
2. **Where each mark came from**, quoted from your answer
3. **What the next mark specifically needed**
4. **Content missing, content not credited, and time, separated**
5. The two changes that would gain the most marks next time
6. What you did well and should keep doing

## What this will not do

It helps you write **your own** answers better. **It will not write an answer for you to submit.** Not an assignment, a project, an internal, or a take-home paper.

The reason is not moralising. An answer you did not write teaches you nothing, and the marks you get for it are the last ones you will get on that topic. The gap it papers over shows up in the exam hall, where nothing can help.

## The command word decides everything

| Command | What earns the marks |
|---|---|
| **State, list, name** | The item. Nothing else earns anything |
| **Define** | Precision, usually a specific form of words |
| **Describe** | What it is, in order, with features |
| **Explain** | The mechanism and the *because*. A description scores badly |
| **Derive, prove** | Every step. A correct final answer with steps missing loses most of the marks |
| **Compare** | Both sides against the same stated criteria. Two separate descriptions score badly |
| **Discuss, evaluate** | Both directions and a position. One-sided answers cap out |
| **Justify** | A claim plus the reason, and the reason carries the marks |

## Written so a tired examiner cannot miss the marks

An examiner marking two hundred scripts either finds a mark or does not. So: one point per line or short paragraph rather than five inside one; the direct answer first, then elaboration; the key term headed or underlined where allowed; a diagram where it is faster than words, labelled and referred to; steps numbered where sequence matters.

**The best answer is not the most impressive one.** It is the one whose marks are findable in eight seconds.

There is also an explicit list of what to cut: introductions restating the question, definitions of terms the question already used, everything you know that was not asked, repetition in the conclusion. That is where most wasted time in a paper goes, and students protect it because it feels like effort.

## The three losses, and why the second one matters most

| Loss | The fix |
|---|---|
| **Content missing** | You did not know it. A study problem |
| **Content present, not credited** | You knew it and wrote it in a way that did not score |
| **Time** | You ran out, or spent it elsewhere |

**The second is the one worth finding.** It is the fastest marks a student can gain, it requires no new knowledge, and it is completely invisible without somebody pointing at it. Most students assume every lost mark is the first category, and study harder at something they already knew.

## What good output looks like

A strong review quotes your own words back and says exactly what the next mark needed. "You have the definition and not the condition; the second mark wanted 'at constant temperature'" is actionable in a way that "be more precise" is not.

In structure mode the useful discomfort is the length arithmetic, since most students write substantially more than the marks justify and have never been told what that costs. If a review says most of your loss was content not credited, that is good news delivered awkwardly: you knew more than your score suggests, and the fix is a writing habit rather than a month of study.

## Using it

Give it the question with its marks, and your attempt.

Examples that work:

- "Five-mark question, 'explain', here is my answer. Where did I lose marks?"
- "How should a 15-mark answer on this be structured? Marking scheme attached."
- "I write a lot and score average. Show me what to cut."
- "My teacher says I do not answer the question. Here are three of my answers."
- "This is a 'compare' question and I wrote two paragraphs. What went wrong?"

Quoting the command word exactly as printed changes the answer more than anything else you can add.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- **It does not write answers for you to submit.** It shows structure and reviews your attempt.
- It does not know your board's or university's marking conventions unless you supply the scheme. Where you do, it applies yours.
- It does not guarantee marks. Examiners vary, and it tells you what a scheme rewards.
- It does not teach the topic. Where the loss is content missing, that is a study problem and it says so.
- It cannot see handwriting, presentation, or a diagram you describe in words rather than draw.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It will not write the answer | Deliberate, and not negotiable | Write an attempt and it will review it properly. |
| Suggested answer shorter than yours | Length tied to marks | Writing beyond the marks costs the questions you never reached. |
| It says you answered a different question | Command word mismatch | The commonest cause of correct material scoring badly. |
| Marks stricter than your teacher's | No scheme supplied, general standard applied | Paste your marking scheme and it applies yours. |
| Most loss is "not credited" | You knew it, and it was buried | Good news. The fix is a writing habit, not a month of study. |
| No diagram feedback | It cannot see one you described in words | Diagrams have to be drawn, and it says what yours must show. |
