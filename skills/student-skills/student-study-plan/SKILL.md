# Study Plan and Revision Schedule

The plan is a timetable with subjects in coloured blocks, made on a Sunday and abandoned by Wednesday. It fails for the same three reasons every time: it assumes more hours than exist, it covers everything once instead of covering the important things repeatedly, and it has no room for the week when something goes wrong.

## When to use this

Trigger on: study plan, timetable, revision schedule, exam prep plan, spaced repetition, revision cycles, "how do I cover the syllabus", "I have two months", "I am behind", "how should I study".

## Before you start

Required: what has to be covered, and the date it has to be ready by.

Ask, briefly:
1. **The hours you actually have, not the hours in a day.** School or college, travel, coaching, meals, sleep, and the things you will not stop doing. The number that comes out of this is almost always half what was assumed, and every failed plan starts here.
2. What you already know, roughly, by topic. A plan treating known and unknown material identically wastes most of its time.
3. Whether an exam analysis exists, or where the marks actually are. Without it, time gets allocated by syllabus size rather than by return.
4. **What has failed before.** Every student has abandoned a plan, the reason is usually the same one, and it can be designed around.
5. Fixed points: tests, submissions, festivals, family commitments.

## Workflow

### 1. Do the honest hours arithmetic first, and show it

Total the hours genuinely available between now and the date. Subtract sleep, fixed commitments, travel, and the hours realistically lost to fatigue. Then compare against what the syllabus needs.

**Where the two do not meet, say so before producing a plan.** A schedule that quietly assumes fourteen productive hours a day is not a plan, it is a way of feeling prepared for a week.

Where there is not enough time, name all three honest options and their consequences: cover less and cover it properly, cover everything shallowly and accept a lower ceiling, or move the date.

### 2. Allocate by return, not by syllabus size

Time goes to topics by marks available and by how far you currently are from getting them. That is not chapter length, and it is not what you enjoy studying.

State plainly which topics get less than proportional time, and why.

### 3. Build revision cycles, not a single pass

The commonest structural failure is a plan that covers everything once and finishes the day before the exam, which means everything from month one is gone.

Plan at least three passes, each shorter and more selective:

- **First pass:** learn it, with practice
- **Second pass:** retrieve it from memory, and re-learn only what fails
- **Third pass:** the failures from pass two, plus the highest-weight material

**The gaps between passes matter more than the length of each.** Spacing is what moves material into memory that survives, and covering a topic three times in one week is close to studying it once.

### 4. Schedule retrieval, not re-reading

Re-reading feels productive and produces exactly the recognition feeling that an understanding check exists to catch.

Every session ends with something retrieved from memory: a question attempted without notes, a derivation reproduced, a definition written from scratch. State this per session, and state the rule plainly: **if you did not close the book, it was not revision.**

### 5. Interleave practice rather than blocking it

Studying one topic for six hours feels efficient and produces worse retention and worse discrimination between problem types than three topics for two hours each. The discomfort is the point; it is what makes the recall effortful enough to stick.

Do not interleave first exposure. Interleave practice and revision.

### 6. Build in the failure the plan will meet

Every plan meets a week where something goes wrong:

- Empty catch-up slots, at least one a week, unallocated
- **What to drop first when time is lost, decided now**
- A rule for what happens when a day is missed, so the plan does not need rebuilding every time

A plan with no slack ends the first time life happens, and its failure feels like a personal failure rather than a design one.

### 7. Make sessions specific enough to start

"Study chemistry" is not a session, and it produces ten minutes of deciding what to do. "Chemical kinetics, worked examples 4 to 12, then the 2019 questions without notes" starts immediately.

### 8. Set the weekly checkpoint

Not to feel bad about what was missed. To answer three questions: what is actually going in, what is not, and what changes next week. Put those three questions in the plan.

## Output format

**1. The hours arithmetic.** Available against required, stated plainly.

**2. The verdict on whether it fits**, and the options where it does not.

**3. Allocation.** Time per topic with the reason, and what gets less than proportional time.

**4. Revision cycles.** Three passes, with the spacing between them.

**5. The schedule.** Sessions specific enough to start without deciding.

**6. Retrieval per session.** What gets closed-book, and when.

**7. Slack.** Catch-up slots, and what gets dropped first.

**8. The rule for a missed day.**

**9. The weekly checkpoint.** Three questions.

## Quality bar

- Is the hours arithmetic done and shown before any schedule is produced
- Where the time does not fit, is that said before the plan rather than buried inside it
- Is time allocated by return rather than by syllabus size
- Are there at least three revision passes, with stated spacing between them
- Does every session end with something retrieved from memory rather than re-read
- Is practice interleaved rather than blocked
- Is there unallocated catch-up time, at least weekly
- Is what to drop first decided in advance rather than at the moment of losing time
- Is there a rule for a missed day, so the plan survives one
- Is every session specific enough to start without deciding what to do

## What this skill does not do

- It does not know how fast you learn. It plans against the estimate you give, and says when that estimate looks optimistic.
- It does not tell you to drop a subject or a topic. It shows what the time allows and what leaving something would cost.
- It does not predict a score or a rank.
- It cannot make you follow it. It designs for the week you miss, which is the most useful thing a plan can do.
- It does not replace an exam analysis. Without knowing where the marks are it allocates by syllabus size, which is the thing it is trying to avoid.
