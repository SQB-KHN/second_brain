# Study Plan and Revision Schedule

Does the honest hours arithmetic before writing any schedule, allocates time by return rather than syllabus size, and builds three spaced revision passes with room for the week that goes wrong.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone with a syllabus and a deadline: class 9 to 12 students, competitive exam aspirants across engineering, medical, civil services, management and banking, undergraduates and postgraduates, working professionals studying part-time, and anyone who has abandoned three plans already.

## The problem it solves

The plan is a timetable with subjects in coloured blocks, made on a Sunday and abandoned by Wednesday.

It fails for the same three reasons every time, and none of them is discipline. It assumes more hours than exist, because nobody subtracts travel, coaching, meals and the fatigue that makes the ninth hour worthless. It covers everything once instead of covering the important things repeatedly, so month one is gone by month three. And it has no room for the week when something goes wrong, so the first missed day breaks the whole structure and the failure feels personal rather than structural.

The learning science that fixes this has been settled for decades and almost nobody applies it. Spacing beats massing. Retrieval beats re-reading, by a wide margin. Interleaved practice beats blocked practice. Every one of these feels worse while you are doing it, which is exactly why students avoid them and why plans keep being built the comfortable way.

---

## What you give it

**Minimum:** what has to be covered, and the date it has to be ready by.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **The hours you actually have** | Not hours in a day. After school, travel, coaching, meals and sleep. Usually half what was assumed, and every failed plan starts here |
| What you already know, roughly by topic | A plan treating known and unknown material identically wastes most of its time |
| **Where the marks actually are** | Without it, time gets allocated by syllabus size, which is the thing this is trying to avoid |
| **What has failed before** | Every student has abandoned a plan, the reason is usually the same one, and it can be designed around |
| Fixed points | Tests, submissions, festivals, family commitments |

## What you get back

1. **The hours arithmetic**, available against required, before any schedule
2. A verdict on whether it fits, and the honest options where it does not
3. Allocation by return, with what gets less than proportional time and why
4. **Three revision passes with the spacing between them**
5. Sessions specific enough to start without deciding what to do
6. **What gets retrieved from memory in each session**
7. Slack: catch-up slots, and what gets dropped first
8. A rule for a missed day, so the plan survives one
9. A weekly checkpoint, as three questions

## The arithmetic comes before the schedule

Total the hours genuinely available. Subtract sleep, fixed commitments, travel and the hours realistically lost to fatigue. Compare against what the syllabus needs.

**Where the two do not meet, it says so before producing a plan.** A schedule quietly assuming fourteen productive hours a day is not a plan, it is a way of feeling prepared for about a week.

Where there is not enough time, you get three honest options with their consequences: cover less and cover it properly, cover everything shallowly and accept a lower ceiling, or move the date. Nobody enjoys that conversation and it is the most useful part of the output.

## Three passes, and the gaps matter more than the passes

| Pass | What it does |
|---|---|
| **First** | Learn it, with practice |
| **Second** | Retrieve it from memory. Re-learn only what fails |
| **Third** | The failures from pass two, plus the highest-weight material |

The commonest structural failure is a plan covering everything once, finishing the day before the exam, which means everything from month one has gone.

**Spacing is what moves material into memory that survives.** Covering a topic three times in one week is close to studying it once, and it feels far more productive, which is the trap.

## If you did not close the book, it was not revision

Re-reading feels productive and produces exactly the recognition feeling that leads students to walk into exams confident and blank.

So every session in the plan ends with something retrieved: a question attempted without notes, a derivation reproduced, a definition written from scratch. It is stated per session, and the rule is stated plainly, because the alternative is comfortable and does not work.

Practice also gets **interleaved rather than blocked**. Six hours on one topic feels efficient and produces worse retention and worse discrimination between problem types than two hours each on three. First exposure is not interleaved; practice and revision are.

## It plans for the week you will miss

Every plan meets a week where something goes wrong. So this one contains, deliberately:

- Unallocated catch-up slots, at least one a week
- **What to drop first when time is lost, decided now** rather than at the moment of losing it
- A rule for a missed day, so the plan does not need rebuilding every time

A plan with no slack ends the first time life happens, and its failure gets experienced as a personal failure rather than a design one. That is why students stop making plans.

## What good output looks like

A strong plan is uncomfortable in the first section and reassuring afterwards. The hours available are usually far fewer than expected, and seeing the number is what makes the rest of the plan believable.

Sessions should be specific enough that you start immediately. "Study chemistry" produces ten minutes of deciding what to do; "chemical kinetics, worked examples 4 to 12, then the 2019 questions without notes" does not.

If the plan tells you the syllabus does not fit the time, that is the finding rather than a failure, and it is far better to hear it now than in the last fortnight.

## Using it

Say what has to be covered, by when, and how many hours you genuinely have.

Examples that work:

- "Class 12 boards in 90 days. School until 3, coaching 5 to 8. Six subjects."
- "I have abandoned three timetables. Build one that survives a bad week."
- "Two months to the exam and I have covered 40 percent. What are my options?"
- "I re-read notes and forget everything. Build a plan around retrieval."
- "Weightage analysis attached. Allocate my time against it."

Being honest about the hours, rather than optimistic, is what decides whether the plan is worth having.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not know how fast you learn. It plans against your estimate and says when the estimate looks optimistic.
- It does not tell you to drop a subject or a topic. It shows what the time allows and what leaving something would cost.
- It does not predict a score or a rank.
- **It cannot make you follow it.** What it can do is design for the week you miss, which is the most useful thing a plan can do.
- It does not replace an exam analysis. Without knowing where the marks are, it allocates by syllabus size.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Far fewer hours than you thought | Travel, coaching and fatigue subtracted | Usually accurate, and it is the reason previous plans failed. |
| It says the syllabus does not fit | It genuinely does not, in that time | You get three options. All three are better than discovering it in month two. |
| Fewer topics than you wanted | Allocated by return, not by syllabus size | Check what got less time and why. |
| Sessions feel over-specified | Vague sessions cost ten minutes of deciding | That is the point. You start immediately. |
| Gaps between revision passes look wasteful | Spacing is what makes revision stick | Three passes in one week is close to studying once. |
| Empty slots in the schedule | Deliberate catch-up time | Fill them and the plan breaks the first time life happens. |
