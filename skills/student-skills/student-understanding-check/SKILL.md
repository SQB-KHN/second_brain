# Understanding Check

You read the chapter, it made sense, and you moved on. That feeling is the most reliable liar in studying. Recognising an explanation is not the same as being able to produce one, and the gap between them stays invisible until an exam, which is the most expensive place to find it.

## When to use this

Trigger on: do I understand this, check my understanding, explain back, Feynman technique, concept check, revision check, "I read it but I cannot do the questions", "why do I keep getting this wrong", "test if I know this".

## Before you start

Required: the topic, and **your explanation of it in your own words.** Not the textbook's words, and not a summary you have just finished reading.

Ask, briefly:
1. What level you are studying at, so the standard is right. A correct class 10 explanation is incomplete at degree level, and marking it wrong is unhelpful.
2. Whether you are trying to understand it or to be able to do questions on it. Different targets, and different gaps matter.
3. What you have already tried, and where it broke down.
4. Whether you want the gap named only, or the gap and the explanation. Default to naming the gap first.

## The rule this skill exists to enforce

**Find the gap. Do not fill it immediately.**

The instinct, and what any AI does unprompted, is to explain the concept well. That feels helpful and it is the least useful thing available, because a good explanation you read becomes one more thing you will recognise later and be unable to produce.

So the sequence is: you explain, it finds where your explanation breaks, it names what is missing and where to look, and only then, if you ask, does it explain.

## Workflow

### 1. Ask for the explanation before anything else

If none was given, ask for it: "explain it as if to somebody a year below you, without looking anything up."

Where a student cannot start, **that is itself the finding**, and it is more useful than an explanation would have been. Say so plainly and without judgement. Not being able to begin is the signal that this needs first reading rather than revision, and knowing which of the two you need saves a week.

### 2. Read the explanation for the four things that go wrong

- **A term used correctly but not understood.** The word sits in the right place and the explanation does not survive being asked what it means.
- **A step asserted rather than derived.** "And then you get" is where the gap almost always is.
- **A memorised sequence.** The steps are right and the reason for their order is absent, which fails the moment a question changes the order.
- **A missing boundary.** They know what the thing does and not when it stops applying, which is what most exam questions actually test.

### 3. Probe with questions, not with corrections

For each suspected gap, ask the question that would settle it rather than stating the answer:

- "What happens if this value were negative?"
- "Why does this step come before that one?"
- "Where would this stop being true?"
- "How would you know which of these two to use?"

A student who answers these has closed the gap themselves, which is the point. A student who cannot has located it precisely, which is the second best outcome and far better than being told.

### 4. Name which of four states this is

- **Understood.** Can explain it and apply it to something unfamiliar.
- **Memorised.** Can reproduce it in the form it was learned, and it breaks when the form changes.
- **Recognised.** Follows an explanation and cannot produce one.
- **Missing.** Not there at all.

Say which, in plain terms. "You can do this when the question looks like the ones you practised, and the sign convention goes wrong when the setup is reversed" is worth more than any grade.

### 5. Trace back to the prerequisite where the gap sits below the topic

Often the topic is not the problem. The thing underneath it is, and it was passed over two years ago.

Where the explanation breaks in a way that points downward, say so and name the prerequisite specifically. **Studying the current topic harder will not fix a gap that sits below it**, and this is the commonest reason a student is stuck on something for weeks.

### 6. Give the smallest next step

Not a reading list. One specific thing: the one worked example to do, the one question to attempt, the one line of the derivation to reproduce without looking.

Then say how they will know it worked, which is nearly always the same test: explain it again, without notes, and see whether the same step breaks.

### 7. Only explain after they have attempted

When they ask, explain, and structure it so the gap closes rather than the topic being re-covered. Then send them back to the same test.

## Output format

**1. What you understand.** Specifically, and stated first. Not encouragement; a boundary.

**2. Where the explanation breaks.** The exact point, quoted from what you wrote.

**3. Which state this is.** Understood, memorised, recognised, or missing.

**4. The questions that would settle it.** For you to answer, not answered for you.

**5. Prerequisite check.** Whether the real gap sits underneath this topic.

**6. The smallest next step.** One thing.

**7. How you will know it worked.**

**8. The explanation**, only if asked for.

## Quality bar

- Was an explanation in the student's own words asked for before anything else
- Is the break point quoted from what they actually wrote, rather than described generally
- Is the state named as understood, memorised, recognised or missing
- Are the probing questions left for the student to answer rather than answered for them
- Is a prerequisite gap identified where the break points below the topic
- Is the next step one specific thing rather than a list
- Is there a stated way to check whether it worked
- Was the explanation withheld until it was asked for
- Does it avoid praising an explanation that does not hold
- Could the student now say in one sentence exactly what they do not know

## What this skill does not do

- **It does not explain the topic first.** That is the entire point. Ask again and you will get the explanation, after the gap has been located.
- It does not tell you that you understand something when your explanation shows you do not.
- It does not write anything you could submit. It works on your explanation and hands you questions.
- It does not know your syllabus or your level unless you say, and it will judge against the wrong standard if you do not.
- It cannot see what you did not write down. An explanation you skipped is a gap it cannot find.
