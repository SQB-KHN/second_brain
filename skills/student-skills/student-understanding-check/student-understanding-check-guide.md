# Understanding Check

Takes your own explanation of a topic, finds exactly where it breaks, and tells you whether you understood it, memorised it, or only recognised it. It will not explain the topic until you ask.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who studies and is not sure it is working: school students, competitive exam aspirants, undergraduates and postgraduates, working professionals studying part-time, and anyone who has read a chapter three times and still cannot do the questions.

## The problem it solves

You read the chapter, it made sense, and you moved on.

That feeling is the most reliable liar in studying. Recognising an explanation is not the same as being able to produce one, and the two feel identical from the inside. The gap stays invisible until an exam, which is the most expensive possible place to discover it.

The reason it survives is that every available check makes it worse. Re-reading confirms the feeling. Looking at a worked solution confirms the feeling. And asking any AI produces a clear explanation of the concept, which confirms the feeling most of all, because now you have understood a very good explanation and will recognise it again next week without being able to produce it.

**The only thing that finds the gap is trying to explain it yourself**, which is uncomfortable and therefore skipped.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the topic, and **your explanation of it in your own words.** Not the textbook's, and not a summary you have just read.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Your explanation, written without looking anything up** | The entire input. Nothing else can locate the gap |
| Your level | A correct class 10 explanation is incomplete at degree level, and marking it wrong is unhelpful |
| Whether you want to understand it or do questions on it | Different targets, and different gaps matter |
| What you already tried, and where it broke | Saves it finding a gap you have already found |

If you cannot produce an explanation at all, **that is the result**, and it is more useful than an explanation would have been. It means this needs first reading rather than revision, and knowing which of the two you need saves a week.

## What you get back

1. What you do understand, stated first and specifically. A boundary, not encouragement
2. **Where your explanation breaks**, quoted from what you actually wrote
3. **Which of four states this is**: understood, memorised, recognised, or missing
4. **Questions that would settle it**, for you to answer rather than answered for you
5. A prerequisite check: whether the real gap sits underneath this topic
6. The smallest next step. One thing
7. How you will know it worked
8. The explanation, only if you ask

---

## Why it withholds the explanation

The instinct of any AI asked about a concept is to explain it well. It feels helpful and it is the least useful thing available here, because a good explanation you read becomes one more thing you will recognise later and be unable to produce.

So the sequence is deliberate: you explain, it finds the break, it names what is missing and where to look, and only then, if you ask, does it explain.

If you want the explanation immediately, ask for it and you will get it. The default is the other way round because the default is what most people accept.

## Four states, and they need different work

| State | What it means | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Understood** | Can explain it and apply it to something unfamiliar | Move on |
| **Memorised** | Reproduces the form it was learned in, breaks when the form changes | Varied practice, not re-reading |
| **Recognised** | Follows an explanation, cannot produce one | Explain it out loud until it holds |
| **Missing** | Not there at all | First reading, not revision |

Being told which one you are in is worth more than a grade, because all four feel similar while you are studying and each wastes a different amount of your time if misdiagnosed.

## The four things that go wrong in an explanation

- **A term used correctly but not understood.** The word is in the right place, and the explanation does not survive being asked what it means.
- **A step asserted rather than derived.** "And then you get" is where the gap almost always sits.
- **A memorised sequence.** The steps are right and the reason for their order is absent, which fails the moment a question reorders them.
- **A missing boundary.** You know what the thing does and not when it stops applying, which is what most exam questions actually test.

## Often the gap is not in this topic

Where your explanation breaks in a way that points downward, it names the prerequisite specifically.

**Studying the current topic harder will not fix a gap sitting below it**, and this is the commonest reason a student is stuck on something for weeks and concludes they are bad at the subject.

## What good output looks like

A strong result is uncomfortable and specific. It quotes the exact sentence where your explanation stopped being an explanation, and it usually turns out to be a step you have written a hundred times without examining.

It should hand you questions rather than answers. If you can answer them, you have closed the gap yourself, which is the point. If you cannot, you have located it precisely, which is the second best outcome and much better than being told.

If it says you understand something, that is worth something, because it has no incentive to be encouraging and it will say plainly when an explanation does not hold.

## Installing it

1. Download this folder, or make a folder named `student-understanding-check` and put `SKILL.md` inside it. The guide does not need to go in.
2. Compress the folder to a `.zip`.
3. Go to Customize, then Skills, then add a skill and upload the zip.

Code execution is not required for this skill, so it works on a free plan.

## Using it

Write your explanation first, then paste it.

Examples that work:

- "Here is how I would explain electromagnetic induction. Where does it break?"
- "I can do the standard questions on this and I fail the twisted ones. Why?"
- "Explaining photosynthesis to a class 8 student. Check my version."
- "I have read this chapter three times and cannot do the exercises."
- "Do not explain it yet. Just tell me what I am missing."

That last one is the default anyway, and saying it makes the intent clear.

---

## Limits worth knowing

- **It does not explain the topic first.** That is the entire point. Ask again and you will get it, after the gap has been located.
- It does not tell you that you understand something when your explanation shows you do not.
- It does not write anything you could submit. It works on your explanation and hands you questions.
- It does not know your syllabus or your level unless you say, and it will judge against the wrong standard if you do not.
- It cannot see what you did not write down. An explanation you skipped is a gap it cannot find.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It will not explain the concept | Deliberate, and the whole design | Ask directly and you will get it, after the gap is located. |
| It says you only memorised it | Your explanation reproduced a form without the reasons | Uncomfortable and usually right. Try explaining the order. |
| It asks questions instead of answering | Answering them closes the gap yourself | If you cannot answer, that is the finding. Say so. |
| It sent you to an earlier topic | The break pointed below this one | Studying this topic harder will not fix a gap underneath it. |
| It says you cannot start | You could not produce an explanation | That is the result. You need first reading, not revision. |
| Judged too harshly | Your level was not stated | Say the class or course. It judges against the wrong standard otherwise. |
