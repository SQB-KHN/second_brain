# Voice Guide and Consistency Check

Almost every brand describes its voice as friendly, professional and approachable, which describes no voice at all and rules nothing out. So each writer guesses, output drifts, and every review turns into an argument nobody can settle, because there is no written standard to appeal to. This produces the standard, and then checks work against it.

## When to use this

Trigger on: brand voice, tone of voice, style guide, voice guidelines, house style, editorial standards, writer onboarding, "our content sounds inconsistent", "does this sound like us", "make this sound like our brand".

## Before you start

Required: samples of writing that already is the voice. Five to ten pieces the brand is happy with, published or not.

Ask, briefly:
1. **Which samples are on-voice and which are off.** The off-voice ones are more useful, because a voice is defined as much by what it rejects as by what it does.
2. Who writes: one person, a team, freelancers, or an agency. A guide for one person can be short. A guide for six freelancers has to be explicit and testable.
3. Any voice the brand is deliberately not. A competitor whose tone they dislike, or a register they have moved away from.
4. Constraints already fixed: regulated language, terms legal has banned, spellings and conventions in use.

## Workflow

### 1. Derive the voice from samples, do not describe it with adjectives

Adjectives are where voice guides die. "Confident" is not actionable, and two writers will produce opposite work from it. Extract observable patterns instead:

- Sentence length distribution, and whether short sentences are used for emphasis
- Whether the brand says you, we, I, or uses the passive
- Contractions or not
- How a piece opens: a claim, a question, a story, or a number
- Whether it uses humour, and of what kind: understatement, self-deprecation, wordplay, none
- Whether it uses jargon, and exactly where that line sits
- How it handles bad news, uncertainty, and its own limitations
- Punctuation habits: dashes, semicolons, exclamation marks, single-sentence paragraphs
- Whether it makes a claim first or shows evidence first

Quote the sample lines each pattern rests on. A pattern with no example behind it is an opinion.

### 2. Write every rule as a testable pair

This is the mechanism that makes a guide usable rather than decorative. Every rule gets a do and a not, both concrete:

| Rule | Do | Not |

A rule without a "not" cannot be applied. "Be concise" is not a rule. "Cut the first sentence of every draft, because it is almost always throat-clearing" is a rule.

### 3. Build the lexicon

- Words we use, with the reason
- **Words we never use, with the reason.** This is the section people actually check.
- Terminology decisions: which of two synonyms is house standard
- Product and feature naming, capitalisation, and whether the article is used
- Spelling and conventions: which English, date format, number format, Oxford comma or not
- How to refer to the reader, to the company, and to competitors

### 4. Define the register range, not a single register

A brand does not have one voice. It has a range, and the failure is applying the wrong point of it. Define the ends:

- Where it is most formal, such as a contract, an incident notice or a regulatory communication, and what changes there
- Where it is most casual, such as a social reply, and what changes there
- **What stays constant across the whole range.** That constant is the actual voice, and naming it is the most useful thing in the document.

### 5. Write the anti-patterns

The section a team reads most often. Name the specific failures this brand falls into, drawn from the off-voice samples:

- The phrases that keep appearing and should not
- The structural habit, such as opening with a definition or burying the point in paragraph four
- The register slip, such as going corporate under pressure or flippant about something serious

General writing advice does not belong here. If a line could appear in any style guide, it is not an anti-pattern for this brand.

### 6. Make it checkable in two minutes

End with five to eight questions a writer or reviewer can run quickly, each answerable yes or no, each traceable to a rule above. A guide nobody can apply under deadline does not get applied.

### 7. Consistency check mode

When given a draft plus an existing guide, run the draft against it:

- Rule by rule, pass or fail, with the offending line quoted
- Rewrites that fix the rule without changing the argument
- Register: which point of the range this draft sits at, and whether that is right for its context
- **Anything the guide does not cover, flagged as a gap in the guide rather than a fault in the draft**

That last one matters. A draft that breaks no written rule and still feels wrong means the guide is incomplete, and saying so improves the standard instead of blaming the writer.

## Output format

**1. The voice in one paragraph.** Not adjectives. What this brand does that others do not.

**2. Observable patterns.** Each with the sample lines it rests on.

**3. Rules.**

| Rule | Do | Not |

**4. Lexicon.** Words we use, words we never use, terminology decisions, conventions.

**5. Register range.** The formal end, the casual end, and the constant.

**6. Anti-patterns.** Specific to this brand, from the off-voice samples.

**7. Two-minute checklist.**

**8. Gaps.** What the samples could not settle, and what would settle it.

In consistency check mode: rule-by-rule result, quoted lines, rewrites, register assessment, and guide gaps.

## Quality bar

- Is every rule stated as a do and a not, both concrete
- Does every observable pattern quote a sample line
- Would two different writers produce similar work from this guide
- Does the "words we never use" section contain words the brand currently uses
- Is the register range defined at both ends, with the constant named
- Are the anti-patterns specific to this brand rather than general writing advice
- Could a reviewer run the checklist in two minutes
- Does it avoid the words friendly, professional, approachable and authentic entirely

## What this skill does not do

- It does not invent a voice. It derives one from samples, and says where the samples conflict.
- It does not write your content. It produces the standard content gets written against.
- It does not decide your positioning or your claims. It works downstream of those.
- It does not settle a genuine disagreement between stakeholders about what the brand sounds like. It shows what the samples support and names the disagreement.
- It cannot clear anything legally. Banned language you supply is applied; it does not judge what should be banned.
