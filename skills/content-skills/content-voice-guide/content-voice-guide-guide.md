# Voice Guide and Consistency Check

Derives a testable brand voice guide from writing you already have, then checks any draft against it rule by rule.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone responsible for what a brand sounds like: content leads and managers, brand and communications managers, copywriters and content writers, agency strategists building one per client, freelancers matching several clients at once, and founders whose own voice is the brand's.

## The problem it solves

Almost every voice guide says the brand is friendly, professional and approachable. That describes no voice at all, and more importantly it rules nothing out, so it cannot be used to reject anything.

The consequence is predictable. Each writer guesses, the output drifts, and every review becomes an argument about taste that nobody can settle, because there is no written standard to appeal to. Then a new writer joins and the drift restarts.

Building a real guide by hand means reading twenty pieces closely, extracting the patterns, writing rules with counter-examples, and arguing the lexicon into place. It takes a week, so it does not happen, and the adjectives version gets circulated instead.

This derives the guide from the samples in one pass, and then does the more valuable half: checks drafts against it, so the standard actually gets applied.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** five to ten pieces of writing that already are the voice. Published pages, emails, posts, a founder's own words, anything the brand points at and says "that is us".

| Extra input | What it unlocks |
|---|---|
| **Off-voice samples** | The single most useful input. A voice is defined as much by what it rejects, and the anti-patterns section comes almost entirely from these |
| Who writes: one person, a team, freelancers | A guide for one person can be short. For six freelancers it has to be explicit and testable |
| A voice the brand is deliberately not | Sharpens every rule, because a contrast makes a pattern visible |
| Banned terms, regulated language, conventions in use | Applied as fixed constraints rather than proposed |
| An existing guide plus a draft | Switches it into consistency check mode, which is where most of the recurring value sits |

## What you get back

1. The voice in one paragraph, with no adjectives in it
2. **Observable patterns**, each quoting the sample lines it rests on
3. **Rules as do-and-not pairs**, both sides concrete
4. Lexicon: words we use, words we never use, terminology decisions, conventions
5. **Register range**: the formal end, the casual end, and what stays constant across both
6. Anti-patterns specific to this brand, drawn from the off-voice samples
7. A two-minute checklist a reviewer can actually run
8. Gaps: what the samples could not settle, and what would settle them

In consistency check mode: rule-by-rule pass or fail with the offending line quoted, rewrites that fix the rule without changing the argument, a register assessment, and any gap in the guide the draft exposed.

---

## Why it refuses to use adjectives

"Confident" is not actionable. Two writers will produce opposite work from it and both will defend their reading.

So it extracts what is observable instead: sentence length distribution, whether the brand says you or we, contractions or not, how it opens a piece, whether it uses humour and of what kind, where the jargon line sits, how it handles bad news and its own limitations, punctuation habits, and whether it claims first or shows evidence first.

Every pattern quotes the sample line it came from. A pattern with no example behind it is somebody's opinion about the brand, which is what you already had.

The quality bar includes an explicit test: the output must not contain the words friendly, professional, approachable or authentic anywhere.

## Rules only work as pairs

| Not a rule | A rule |
|---|---|
| Be concise | Cut the first sentence of every draft. It is almost always throat-clearing |
| Sound human | Use contractions. "We're" not "we are", including in error messages |
| Be authoritative | Lead with the number, then the interpretation. Never the reverse |
| Avoid jargon | "Onboarding" is fine, "operationalise" is not. The test is whether a customer would say it |

A rule without a "not" cannot be applied to a draft, which is why most guides get read once and never opened again.

## The register range matters more than the voice

A brand does not have one voice. It has a range, and almost every real failure is applying the wrong point of it: going corporate in a social reply, or flippant in an incident notice.

So the guide defines both ends and, more usefully, **what stays constant across the whole range**. That constant is the actual voice. Everything else is register.

## What good output looks like

A strong guide is uncomfortable. The "words we never use" section contains words currently on the website, and the anti-patterns section names a habit the team recognises immediately.

Two different writers should be able to produce similar work from it, which is the only test that matters. If it reads as pleasant and nobody would change anything about their next draft after reading it, the samples were too few or all on-voice.

In check mode, a strong result sometimes says the guide is wrong rather than the draft. A draft that breaks no written rule and still feels off means the guide is incomplete, and saying so improves the standard instead of blaming the writer.

## Installing it

1. Download this folder, or make a folder named `content-voice-guide` and put `SKILL.md` inside it. The guide does not need to go in.
2. Compress the folder to a `.zip`.
3. Go to Customize, then Skills, then add a skill and upload the zip.

Code execution is not required for this skill, so it works on a free plan.

## Using it

Paste the samples and say which are on-voice.

Examples that work:

- "Here are eight of our posts. Three are on-voice, five are not. Build the guide."
- "New freelancer starts Monday. Turn our best content into something they can follow."
- "Does this draft sound like us? Here is the draft and our guide."
- "We sound like every other company in our category. Work out what is actually ours."
- "Our founder writes brilliantly and nobody else can match it. Codify how they write."

Including the off-voice samples roughly doubles the usefulness of the output.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not invent a voice. It derives one from what you supply, and says where the samples conflict with each other.
- It does not write your content. It produces the standard content is written against.
- It does not decide positioning or claims. Those sit upstream, and this works with whatever they are.
- It cannot settle a genuine stakeholder disagreement about what the brand sounds like. It shows what the samples support and names the disagreement.
- It applies banned language you supply. It does not judge what should be banned.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Guide feels generic | Samples were few, or all on-voice | Add off-voice samples. The contrast is what makes patterns visible. |
| Rules you disagree with | The samples genuinely support them | Check which sample line each rests on. Usually the sample was off-voice and unlabelled. |
| "Words we never use" is empty | Nothing in the samples contradicted itself | Supply off-voice material, or a competitor whose tone you dislike. |
| Check mode passes a bad draft | The guide does not cover that failure | It reports this as a guide gap. Add the rule and rerun. |
| Too many rules to use | Guide written for a large team | Ask for the two-minute checklist only. That is the version people actually run. |
