# Repurposing and Channel Adaptation

Breaks one asset into the ideas that genuinely stand alone, matches each to the channel whose shape it fits, and rewrites for that channel instead of truncating.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone turning one piece of work into many: content marketers and content leads, social media managers, newsletter writers, video and podcast producers, agency content teams, and solo creators who produce one long thing a week and need it to reach five places.

## The problem it solves

Repurposing usually means the same paragraph pasted into five places with the character count adjusted.

Every channel then underperforms, and the conclusion drawn is that the content was weak. What was actually weak was the translation. Truncation keeps the opening and loses the point, because in long form the point arrives after the setup, and in short form it has to arrive first. So the derivative that gets published is the setup with the payoff cut off.

The second failure is volume. A team decides one webinar should produce thirty assets, so it produces thirty, and twenty-six of them are weak. Weak derivatives are not free. They cost attention on channels where attention is the only currency.

Doing this properly means finding the ideas that actually stand alone, being honest that there are four rather than thirty, and rewriting each one natively. That takes real editorial judgement, which is why it usually gets skipped in favour of pasting.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the source asset. A blog post, report, webinar transcript, podcast episode, deck, or long interview.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Which channels actually matter** | A list of eight usually means none is being done properly, and it will say so |
| Who runs each channel, and what capacity exists | A plan requiring video nobody can edit is not a plan |
| Whether the source is published, and where | A derivative competing with its own source in search is self-inflicted |
| What the source was for | Derivatives can share that goal or serve a different one, and the difference changes the rewriting |
| A voice guide, if one exists | Register gets adjusted per channel while staying inside your range |

**It handles:** raw transcripts with filler and crosstalk, decks with speaker notes, long reports, and interviews where the good material is buried in the middle.

## What you get back

1. **Atomic ideas**, with an honest count and what makes each one stand alone
2. **Idea to channel map**, with the reason for every placement and who produces it
3. The derivatives, each written for its channel rather than cut down
4. **Do not repurpose**: what stays whole, and why
5. Sequence: order, spacing, and which ones link back to the source
6. Canonical note, where derivatives could compete with the source
7. What to watch per channel, and what would mean the idea does not travel there
8. Capacity check: what the plan needs against what you said you have

## The honest count is the whole discipline

Most long assets contain three to six ideas that genuinely stand alone. Not thirty.

An idea stands alone if a reader could take it, understand it and use it without the rest of the piece. Everything else in the source is support: the setup, the second example, the caveat, the transition.

So the first thing it does is state the real count. An asset with four ideas generates four strong derivatives, and being told that is more useful than being handed forty. The forty version is the one that makes a channel look like it does not work.

## Channels reward different shapes

| Channel form | The idea that fits |
|---|---|
| **Long text**: newsletter, article | Needs an argument, and can carry a worked example |
| **Short text**: a single post | One idea, one proof, one line of consequence. Two proofs will not fit |
| **Carousel or slides** | A sequence, a process, or a comparison. Not a paragraph across ten slides |
| **Short video** | One idea with something to show. If it has to be explained rather than demonstrated, it does not belong |
| **Long video or podcast** | The argument with the digressions kept, which is exactly what the text version cut |
| **Visual or chart** | A relationship between numbers. Not a list with a background colour |

An idea that fits no channel's shape is not adapted for that channel. It leaves the slot empty and says so, rather than filling it with something weak.

## Rewriting, not truncating

For every derivative, four things change:

**The hook gets restructured.** Long form earns attention with its first paragraph. Short form has to earn it in the first line, and that line is very often a sentence from the middle of the source.

**The ending gets rebuilt.** A blog post ends with a synthesis. A post ends with something a reader can respond to.

**Evidence density changes.** Long form supports a claim with three examples. Short form uses the best one and cuts the rest.

**Register shifts to the channel**, while staying inside the brand's range rather than stepping outside it.

And no derivative carries a reference to the source's own structure. "As we discussed above" reveals a paste, and readers notice immediately.

## What good output looks like

A strong result tells you the count is four when you were hoping for thirty, and leaves at least one channel slot empty because nothing in the source fits its shape. Both of those are the skill working.

The "do not repurpose" section should have something in it. Anything whose value is its completeness, a methodology or a full comparison, produces something misleading when fragmented, and somebody has to say so.

Every short derivative should carry its point in the first line. If any of them opens with setup, it was truncated rather than rewritten, and that is the one failure this exists to prevent.

## Installing it

1. Download this folder, or make a folder named `content-repurposing` and put `SKILL.md` inside it. The guide does not need to go in.
2. Compress the folder to a `.zip`.
3. Go to Customize, then Skills, then add a skill and upload the zip.

Code execution is not required for this skill, so it works on a free plan.

## Using it

Paste the source and say which channels you actually run.

Examples that work:

- "Webinar transcript, 50 minutes. We run LinkedIn and a newsletter. Nothing else."
- "This report took a month. Get everything out of it that is genuinely worth posting."
- "Podcast episode. What can become short video, and what cannot?"
- "Turn this blog post into a newsletter. Do not just paste it."
- "We publish one long piece a week and post daily. Build the week from this."

Saying what capacity you have stops it planning derivatives nobody can produce.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not schedule or publish anything.
- It does not add new claims or evidence to a derivative. Everything traces back to the source, and where a derivative needs support the source lacks, that becomes a query.
- It does not produce video, audio or design. It specifies what each has to contain and do.
- It does not promise reach or engagement, for any derivative on any channel.
- It will not fill a channel it has no material for. A weak derivative costs more than no derivative.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Far fewer derivatives than expected | The source genuinely holds fewer standalone ideas | That is the finding. Four strong ones beat thirty weak ones. |
| A channel came back empty | Nothing in the source fits its shape | Deliberate. Forcing it produces the derivative that makes the channel look dead. |
| Derivatives feel repetitive | Two atomic ideas were close enough to be one | Say so and it merges them, which usually improves both. |
| No numbers on expected performance | It will not predict them | You get the metric to watch and what failure would look like. |
| Plan needs work you cannot do | Capacity not stated | Tell it who runs what and how much time exists. |
