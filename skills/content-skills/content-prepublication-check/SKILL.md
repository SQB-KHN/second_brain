# Pre-publication Check

The draft is finished, the deadline is now, and the person about to publish is the person who wrote it. So the checks that get skipped are exactly the ones only an outsider would run: does every number have a source, does the headline promise what the body actually delivers, and is that unusually fluent paragraph somebody else's work.

## When to use this

Trigger on: pre-publication check, fact check, before I publish, claim check, sources, editorial review, "is this ready to publish", "check this before it goes out", proofing, compliance check.

## Before you start

Required: the draft.

Ask, briefly:
1. Where it publishes and under whose name. A bylined expert piece, a brand blog and a regulated communication carry different standards.
2. Whether a voice guide, style guide or approved claims list exists. If so, apply theirs rather than a general standard.
3. **Whether any part of the draft was AI-generated or assembled from other sources.** Ask plainly and without judgement. The answer decides where to look hardest, and it is the most useful thing you can be told.
4. Any regulated territory: health, finance, legal, employment, children, or a claim about a named competitor.

## Workflow

### 1. Build the claim register before reading for quality

Extract every factual assertion in the draft into a register. A factual assertion is anything a reader could check and find wrong: a number, a date, a percentage, a comparison, an attribution, a superlative, a causal statement, or a description of what somebody else does.

For each one, record the exact sentence, the type of claim, and its source status.

Doing this mechanically, as a list, separated from reading the piece, is the point. It is what surfaces the claims that read as harmless background and are in fact assertions nobody has checked.

### 2. Assign a source status to every claim, and be strict

- **Sourced.** A named, checkable source is cited or supplied.
- **Sourceable.** True and checkable, source not yet cited.
- **Internal.** Rests on the brand's own data, which then has to be stated as the brand's own.
- **Experience.** Rests on something the brand did. **Interrogate this one hardest**, because content routinely claims experience nobody had. "We tested 40 tools" requires somebody to have tested 40 tools.
- **Unsupported.** No source exists or could be found.
- **Unverifiable.** Cannot be checked either way.

Then state what has to happen to each: cite it, soften it to something that is true, or cut it. An unsupported claim in a published piece is not a style problem.

### 3. Interrogate the numbers specifically

Numbers get trusted more than sentences and checked less often:

- Does the number appear in the cited source at all, or is it an approximation of something adjacent
- Is it current, and is its year stated
- **Is a percentage's base stated?** "Conversion rose 30 percent" from 1 to 1.3 percent is a very different claim from 30 to 39 percent
- Is a total consistent with its parts everywhere it appears in the piece. Check this in code, not by reading; a figure that is right in paragraph two and wrong in paragraph nine is exactly what a reading pass misses
- Is a statistic attributed to a body that would plausibly publish it

Fabricated citations are the characteristic failure of AI-assisted content, and they are fluent, plausible and specific, which is precisely why they get published.

### 4. Check the headline against the body

The promise-and-delivery check, and the most common reason a competent piece disappoints. Treat the headline and every subhead as a promise, and verify the body delivers each one. Report:

- Promises the body does not deliver
- Things the body delivers that the headline does not promise, which is a headline problem rather than a body problem
- Any number in the headline that does not appear in the body

### 5. Look for the originality problem, and state the limit

This is not a plagiarism scanner, and it says so. What it can do:

- Flag passages whose register or specificity differs sharply from the rest of the draft, which is what lifted text looks like
- Flag close paraphrase of a cited source where quoting was the honest option
- Flag uncredited use of somebody else's framework, model or coined term
- Flag phrasings characteristic of another named source, where the draft names one

Recommend a scan with an actual tool wherever the risk is real. Be explicit about what was not checked rather than implying coverage this does not have.

### 6. Raise the compliance flags and route them

Flag, name the owner, and do not rule:

- Health, medical, financial, legal or tax statements that read as advice
- Employment, discrimination or accessibility language
- Claims about a named competitor
- Anything involving children, or aimed at them
- Testimonials and endorsements, and whether a paid relationship is disclosed
- Data and privacy statements

State the flag and who in their organisation resolves it. Do not offer the legal opinion yourself.

### 7. Run the quality pass, and only now

- Does the first paragraph earn the second, or is it throat-clearing
- Is the reader's question answered before the halfway point
- One idea per paragraph
- Specifics where there are currently abstractions
- Hedges that mean nothing: arguably, it could be said, many experts believe
- Voice guide compliance, where one was supplied

Structural notes before line notes. Fixing sentences in a piece whose structure is wrong is wasted work, and it is the order most reviews get backwards.

### 8. Give a verdict, and make it a decision

Ready, ready with fixes, or not ready. **If any unsupported claim survives, it is not ready**, regardless of how good the writing is.

## Output format

**1. Verdict.** Ready, ready with fixes, or not ready, and the blocker count.

**2. Claim register.**

| Sentence | Claim type | Source status | What has to happen |

**3. Number checks.** Each number, its source, and whether it survives.

**4. Headline against body.** Promises kept, broken, and missing.

**5. Originality flags.** With the limits of what was checked stated plainly.

**6. Compliance flags.** Each with the owner it routes to.

**7. Structural notes.** In order of impact.

**8. Line notes.** Only after the structural ones.

**9. What was not checked.** Anything needing access, tooling or expertise this does not have.

## Checks to run before delivering

- Every factual assertion appears in the register, with none skipped as background
- Every register entry carries a source status, with none left blank
- Every number is checked against its cited source, or explicitly marked uncited
- Percentages are checked for a stated base
- The headline and every subhead are tested against the body
- Experience claims are separated from sourced claims and interrogated separately
- The verdict is "not ready" if any unsupported claim remains
- The limits of the originality check are stated rather than implied

## What this skill does not do

- It is not a plagiarism scanner and does not have one. It flags the signals and tells you when to run a real scan.
- It does not give legal, medical, financial or regulatory advice. It flags what needs review and names who should do it.
- It cannot verify a source it cannot see. Where a source is not supplied, the claim is marked uncited rather than assumed true.
- It does not rewrite the piece unless you ask. It tells you what has to change and why.
- It does not decide whether a risk is acceptable. It surfaces the risk and leaves the publishing decision with you.
