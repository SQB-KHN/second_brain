# Content Brief Builder

Turns a vague request into a brief a writer can execute without a second conversation: one question, the reader, the intent, the angle, the structure, and the evidence required before writing starts.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who commissions or receives content work: content leads and managers, editors, content marketers, SEO content strategists, agency account leads briefing writers, freelance writers who have to brief themselves, and founders asking their team for a piece.

## The problem it solves

"We need a blog post about X" is not a brief, and it is what most writers actually receive.

So the writer guesses the audience, guesses the angle, guesses the length, and produces something the requester rejects for reasons that were never written down. The revision cycle that follows is expensive, demoralising, and entirely avoidable, because everything it argues about could have been settled in twenty minutes before anybody wrote a word.

The second failure is quieter. A brief that asks for authority and supplies no evidence pushes a writer on deadline toward inventing a statistic. That is a briefing failure, not a writer failure, and it is how fabricated numbers end up published under a brand's name.

This settles the decisions upfront, in the order that matters, and makes the reviewer's actual criteria explicit rather than leaving them unwritten.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the request, however vague. "We need something on X" is enough to start from.

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Who it is for, and what they already know** | A piece for someone new to the category and one for someone comparing vendors share nothing but the topic |
| **What the reader should do or think differently** | A piece with no intended change cannot be judged, so it gets reviewed on taste instead |
| Where it publishes and gets read | Governs length, form, and how much context has to be carried |
| What already exists internally on this topic | Most teams write a fourth piece where the answer was to update the first |
| Who reviews it, and what has been rejected before | Their real criteria go into the brief instead of staying unwritten |
| What currently ranks or circulates, pasted in | It reads them and says what all of them miss, which is usually the angle |

## What you get back

1. **The one question the piece answers**, written in the reader's words
2. The reader: who, what they know, what they believe, what they would object to
3. **Intent and the structure that follows from it**
4. **The angle**, and the input it rests on
5. Skeleton: what the opening must do, sections in order with what each establishes, the closing ask
6. Length, with the reason it is that length
7. **Evidence required**, each item with its source and status
8. Do-not-say list for this piece
9. Voice reference, and which point of the register range to write at
10. Success measure and the reviewer's actual criteria
11. Open questions, with who can answer each

## Intent decides structure, and getting it wrong wastes the whole piece

Not keyword research. The question is which of four things the reader wants, because each one dictates a different shape.

| Reader wants | Structure that works |
|---|---|
| **To know** | Definition, then mechanism, then implication |
| **To do** | Prerequisites, steps, verification, failure modes |
| **To compare** | Criteria first, then options against those criteria, then a recommendation with its condition |
| **To buy or act** | Proof, objection handling, next step |

A piece that answers a "to do" question with a "to know" structure fails however well it is written, and no amount of editing rescues it. This is the most common structural failure in content, and it is decided in the brief.

## The angle test

The angle is what makes this piece different from the twenty that already exist. There is one test:

**Could a competitor publish this identical piece?** If yes, it is not an angle.

The strongest angles come from something only this brand has: their own data, their own customers' words, a contrarian position they can defend, a process they actually run, or a mistake they actually made.

If the brand has none of those on this topic, the brief says so plainly and gives you three honest options: get the input, narrow the topic to something you do have, or accept this will be a commodity piece and brief it as one. What it will not do is let a generic piece get commissioned as though it were distinctive, then rejected for being generic.

## Evidence gets specified before writing, not after

Every claim the piece has to make is listed, with a status:

- Available internally
- Needs requesting from a named person
- Must be found and cited
- **Unavailable, in which case the claim comes out of the brief now**

That last status is the point. Removing an unsupportable claim at the brief stage costs nothing. Removing it from a finished draft costs a rewrite, and leaving it in costs more than that.

## What good output looks like

A strong brief is longer than the request and shorter than the piece, and it is uncomfortable in two places. The angle section often says the brand has no distinctive input on this topic. The evidence section often removes a claim somebody wanted to make.

The real test is at the bottom of the quality bar: could a freelancer who knows nothing about your company execute this without a second conversation? If not, something in it is still assumed rather than stated.

If the brief comes back thin, the first two questions were answered vaguely. "Marketing managers" is not a reader, and "build awareness" is not a change in the reader.

## Using it

Bring the request exactly as you received it. Vague is fine, and is the point.

Examples that work:

- "My boss wants a post about AI in supply chain. That is all I have been told."
- "Brief this for a freelancer. Comparison piece, our product against two others."
- "Here are the three pieces currently ranking for this query. What should ours do differently?"
- "We keep rejecting drafts on this topic. Write the brief properly this time."
- "I am the writer and there is no brief. Interrogate me and produce one."

That last one is the highest-value use if you work alone.

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not write the piece. It produces the brief the piece is written from.
- It does not do keyword research or estimate search volume. It works out intent and structure from the question, and from whatever you paste in.
- It does not invent the angle's evidence. Where you have no distinctive input, it says so.
- It does not decide positioning or claims. It works downstream of those.
- It cannot see what currently ranks unless you paste it.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Brief feels thin | Reader and intended change answered vaguely | "Marketing managers" is not a reader. Say what they know and believe. |
| It says you have no angle | You genuinely have no distinctive input on this topic | That is the finding. Get the input, narrow the topic, or brief it as commodity. |
| It keeps asking questions | The request has no intended reader change in it | Answer what a reader should do differently. Most other questions resolve from that. |
| Structure looks unfamiliar | It follows from the intent, not from house habit | Check the intent is right. If it is, the structure is too. |
| Length seems short | Justified by what has to fit, not by a target | Ask for the reason. If the reason is wrong, the length changes with it. |
