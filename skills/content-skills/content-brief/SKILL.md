# Content Brief Builder

"We need a blog post about X" is not a brief, and it is what most writers actually receive. So the writer guesses the audience, guesses the angle, guesses the length, and produces something the requester rejects for reasons that were never written down. Then it happens again next week, with a different topic and the same conversation.

## When to use this

Trigger on: content brief, writer brief, blog brief, creative brief, commissioning, freelance brief, article outline, "we need a post about", "what should this piece cover", SEO brief, "brief the writer".

## Before you start

Required: the request, however vague. "We need something on X" is enough to start from.

Ask these, and do not proceed on a guess for the first two:

1. **Who is this for, and what do they already know?** A piece for somebody who has never heard of the category and a piece for somebody comparing two vendors share nothing except the topic.
2. **What should the reader do or think differently afterwards?** If the answer is "be aware of us", push for something a reader could act on. A piece with no intended change cannot be judged, so it gets reviewed on taste instead.
3. Where it publishes and gets read, since that governs length, form and how much context is needed.
4. What already exists on this topic internally. Most teams write a fourth piece on a subject where the answer was to update the first.
5. Who reviews and approves it, and what has been rejected before.

## Workflow

### 1. Establish the one question the piece answers

A piece answers one question. If it answers three, it is three pieces, and each will be worse for sharing the space.

Write the question the way the reader would ask it, in their words. If the question is one nobody would actually ask or search, that is a finding, and it usually means the piece is being written for the company rather than the reader.

### 2. Name the reader's starting point and the gap

- What they already believe
- What they do not know
- What they would object to

The gap between where they start and where the piece leaves them is the actual content. Everything else in a draft is filler, and this is the section that lets a writer cut it with confidence.

### 3. Decide the intent, because it decides the structure

Not keyword research. The question is which of four things the reader wants, and each dictates a different shape:

- **To know.** An explanation. Definition, then mechanism, then implication.
- **To do.** Instructions. Prerequisites, steps, verification, failure modes.
- **To compare.** A decision. Criteria first, then options against those criteria, then a recommendation with the condition under which it holds.
- **To buy or act.** Reassurance. Proof, objection handling, next step.

A piece that answers a "to do" question with a "to know" structure fails regardless of how well it is written. Where the user pastes what currently ranks or circulates, read it and say what all of those pieces miss, because that gap is usually the angle.

### 4. Find the angle, and reject the generic one

The angle is what makes this piece different from the twenty that already exist. Test every candidate against one question: **could a competitor publish this identical piece?** If yes, it is not an angle.

The strongest angles come from something only this brand has: their own data, their own customers' words, a contrarian position they can defend, a process they actually run, or a mistake they actually made.

If the brand has none of these on this topic, say so plainly. The honest options are to get the input, to narrow the topic to something they do have, or to accept this will be a commodity piece and brief it as one. Pretending otherwise produces a draft that gets rejected for being generic, which was decided here and not by the writer.

### 5. Specify structure, not a list of topics

Give the writer the skeleton: what the opening has to do, the sections in order with what each must establish, and what the close asks for. Say what goes in each section rather than naming it, because a section title is not an instruction.

State the length **with its reason**. "1,500 words" is arbitrary and gets padded to. "Long enough to work through three worked examples, so roughly 1,200 to 1,500" is a brief.

### 6. Specify the evidence required before writing starts

List what the piece must cite, and where each item comes from. Mark each as:

- Available internally
- Needs requesting from a named person
- Must be found and cited
- Unavailable, in which case the claim comes out of the brief now

This is the section that prevents the characteristic failure: a writer on deadline invents a statistic because the brief asked for authority and supplied none. That is a briefing failure, not a writer failure.

### 7. Write the do-not-say list for this piece

Claims that cannot be substantiated, competitor comparisons needing legal review, anything the brand has been corrected on before, and any framing this reviewer has already rejected.

### 8. Define what good looks like, before it is written

- The primary measure, and when it gets checked
- **What the reviewer will actually judge it on**, stated explicitly. This is usually unwritten, and it is why drafts come back.
- Two or three specific tests the draft has to pass

## Output format

**1. The one question**, in the reader's words.

**2. Reader.** Who, what they know, what they believe, what they would object to.

**3. Intent and structure.** Which of the four, and the structure that follows from it.

**4. The angle.** What makes this different, and the input it rests on.

**5. Skeleton.** What the opening must do, sections in order with what each establishes, the closing ask.

**6. Length, with the reason.**

**7. Evidence required.** Each item with its source and status.

**8. Do not say.**

**9. Voice reference.** Which point of the register range, and the guide to follow if one exists.

**10. Success measure and reviewer criteria.**

**11. Open questions.** What the requester could not answer, and who can.

## Quality bar

- Does the brief name one question rather than a topic
- Is the reader defined by what they know and believe, not by a job title alone
- Does the specified structure follow from the stated intent
- Would a competitor be unable to publish this same piece
- Does every required claim carry a named source or an explicit status of unavailable
- Is the length justified by what has to fit, rather than asserted
- Are the reviewer's actual criteria written down
- Could a freelancer who knows nothing about the company execute this without a second conversation

## What this skill does not do

- It does not write the piece. It produces the brief the piece gets written from.
- It does not do keyword research or estimate search volume. It works out intent and structure from the question and, where you paste them, from the pieces already ranking.
- It does not invent the angle's evidence. Where the brand has no distinctive input on a topic, it says so and gives you the honest alternatives.
- It does not decide positioning or claims. It works downstream of those.
- It cannot see what currently ranks or circulates unless you paste it in.
