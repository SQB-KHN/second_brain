# Finance Leadership Brief

Finance people are judged on the analysis. They get promoted on the summary. The gap between a correct pack and a decision made because of it is a page of writing, and it is the page most finance professionals were never taught to write.

## When to use this

Trigger on: board pack summary, CEO update, management review notes, "present this to leadership", executive summary of the numbers, "explain this to non-finance people", review meeting prep.

## Before you start

Required: the source analysis. An MIS pack, variance output, cash forecast, or any finance work already done.

Ask, briefly:
1. Who reads it. A CEO, the full board, an investor, or a department head. This changes what leads.
2. What decision is in front of them, if any. A brief written for a decision is a different document from one written for information.
3. Format wanted. One page, a short deck, or an email.
4. Anything they already know, so the brief does not spend its first paragraph on old news.

## The one rule that matters

Lead with the answer.

Finance training builds to a conclusion: context, then method, then analysis, then finding. Leadership reading works in reverse. If the first line does not carry the answer, the reader builds their own conclusion from the first number they see, and the rest of the page is spent correcting it.

Every section starts with its conclusion. Supporting detail follows.

## Workflow

### 1. Find the one thing

Read the source and decide what a leader would do differently after reading it. That is the headline. Everything else supports it or gets cut.

If there is no such thing, say so plainly. "Nothing needs a decision this month, here are the three numbers worth knowing" is an honest and welcome brief. Manufacturing urgency to justify a page is a habit leaders notice.

### 2. Separate signal from noise

Most finance packs contain forty numbers. A brief carries three to five.

Keep a number only if it meets one of these:
- It changes a decision
- It moved enough to need explaining
- It is a number this reader tracks and will look for

Cut everything else. Completeness is the job of the pack. Selection is the job of the brief.

### 3. Translate into business language

The reader thinks in customers, orders, people and cash, not in accounting terms. Translate every time.

- "Gross margin compressed 240 basis points" becomes "we now keep 2.40 rupees less on every 100 rupees of sales"
- "Adverse variance in employee benefit expense" becomes "people costs ran 18 lakh above plan"
- "DSO increased to 68 days" becomes "customers are taking 68 days to pay, up from 54, which is 1.2 crore stuck in receivables"

Notice that each translation adds the consequence. That is the part finance usually leaves out and the part leadership actually needs.

Keep a term only where it is genuinely the shortest accurate way to say something and the reader uses it themselves.

### 4. Make every claim carry a number

"Costs were higher" is not a finding. "Costs ran 42 lakh above plan, of which 31 lakh was the new Pune facility, which was planned but phased late" is.

Every number must trace to the source analysis. Do not introduce a figure that is not in the source, do not round in a way that changes the message, and never estimate to fill a gap. If something matters and the source does not have it, write it as an open item rather than approximating it.

### 5. Name the decisions

This is what separates a brief that gets acted on from one that gets read and forgotten.

Each decision gets: what is being decided, the options, the finance impact of each, what you recommend, who owns it, and by when.

A decision without an owner and a date is information, not a decision. If nobody owns it, either find the owner or move it into the watch list.

## Output format

Use this structure. One page, which is roughly 400 to 500 words. If it runs longer, cut, do not shrink the font.

```
# [Period] Finance Brief

## Where we landed
[Two sentences. The answer. Revenue, profit or cash against plan, and the one thing that explains it.]

## What moved and why
[Three to five items. One short paragraph each. Cause first, number attached, and whether it repeats.]

## What it means for the year
[Two to three sentences. Full-year implication if this continues. This is the section leaders read most closely and finance writes least often.]

## Decisions needed
[Each with options, impact, recommendation, owner and date. If none, say so in one line.]

## Watch list
[Two to four items that need no decision now but would if they continue. One line each.]
```

For a deck, use the same content across five to eight slides: one headline slide, one per major movement, one on full-year implication, one on decisions, one on the watch list. One message per slide, stated in the slide title as a sentence rather than a label. "Margin fell on freight costs" is a title. "Margin Analysis" is not.

## Writing rules

- Short sentences. A leader reads this between meetings.
- No hedging. "Appears to suggest a possible trend" means nothing. State the finding, or state that the data is inconclusive and what would settle it.
- No throat-clearing. Delete "As discussed in the previous review" and start at the finding.
- Active voice with the actor named. "The Pune facility went live a month late" beats "a delay was experienced in commissioning".
- Bad news first and plainly. Leaders discount finance that buries problems, and the discount applies to everything else you write.
- No jargon the reader does not use themselves.

## Quality bar

- Does the first line carry the answer
- Does every number trace to the source, with nothing introduced
- Does every decision have an owner and a date
- Is anything here the reader already knows, and if so, is it cut
- Would a smart non-finance reader understand every sentence on one pass
- Is it one page

## What this skill does not do

- It does not create analysis. It presents analysis that already exists. Give it the source.
- It does not make a number look better than it is. Presentation is not spin.
- It does not decide the recommendation for you where the call is a business judgement. It structures the options and their finance impact.
- It does not send, circulate or file anything. The brief comes back for you to check against your source and put your name on.
- It does not invent a figure the source does not carry. Anything material that is missing becomes an open item rather than an estimate.
