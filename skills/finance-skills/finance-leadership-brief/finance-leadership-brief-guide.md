# Finance Leadership Brief

Turns finance analysis into a one-page brief or short deck that a CEO, board or management team will actually read and act on.

---

## Who this is for

Any finance professional who has to present numbers upward: FP&A analysts preparing review material, management accountants writing board notes, finance managers reporting to a CEO, and finance leads who are the whole finance function and present to founders or investors.

## The problem it solves

Finance professionals are trained to produce complete, correct analysis. Nobody trains them to compress it into the page a leader reads between two meetings. So good work gets presented badly, decisions get made off the first number on the slide, and the analyst who did the work does not get the credit.

It is the least technical skill here and often the one that changes the most. It saves an hour or two per report, but the real return is that your work gets acted on instead of filed.

---

## What you give it

The analysis you have already done.

**Works from:**

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| An MIS or management pack | The most common input |
| Variance analysis or commentary | Pairs naturally with the variance commentary skill |
| A cash flow forecast | Pairs with the cash flow skill |
| A board pack you have drafted | It will compress and restructure it |
| Raw notes from your own analysis | Works, as long as the numbers are yours and final |

**Tell it these four things and the output changes materially:**

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who reads it | A CEO, a board and an investor need different things leading |
| What decision is in front of them | A brief written for a decision is a different document from one for information |
| Format wanted | One page, a short deck, or an email |
| What they already know | Stops the brief spending its opening on old news |

## What you get back

A one-page brief, roughly 400 to 500 words, structured as:

1. **Where we landed.** Two sentences carrying the answer
2. **What moved and why.** Three to five items, cause first, number attached, and whether it repeats
3. **What it means for the year.** The full-year implication, which leaders read most closely and finance writes least often
4. **Decisions needed.** Each with options, impact, recommendation, owner and date
5. **Watch list.** Items that need no decision now but would if they continue

Or the same content as a five to eight slide deck, one message per slide, with each slide title written as a sentence rather than a label.

---

## What it actually does to your writing

**Leads with the answer.** Finance training builds to a conclusion. Leadership reading works in reverse. If the first line does not carry the answer, the reader builds their own from the first number they see and you spend the rest of the page correcting it.

**Cuts forty numbers to four.** A number survives only if it changes a decision, moved enough to need explaining, or is one this reader tracks. Completeness belongs in the pack, not the brief.

**Translates and adds the consequence.** "DSO increased to 68 days" becomes "customers are taking 68 days to pay, up from 54, which is 1.2 crore stuck in receivables". The consequence is the part finance usually leaves out and the part leadership actually needs.

**Forces an owner and a date on every decision.** A decision without those is information. If nobody owns it, it moves to the watch list.

**Puts bad news first and plainly.** Leaders discount finance that buries problems, and the discount then applies to everything else you write.

## What good output looks like

A strong brief answers the question in its first line and fits on one page. Numbers arrive with the consequence attached: "Customers are taking 68 days to pay, up from 54, which is 1.2 crore stuck in receivables." Every decision carries an owner and a date, and the bad news is in the first half.

If it reads generic, you gave it a summary rather than the analysis. If it runs past a page, too much was treated as material, so name your top three and rerun.

## Using it

Attach or paste your analysis and say who it is for.

Examples that work:

- "Turn this MIS pack into a one-page brief for the CEO"
- "I present to the board on Thursday, make this into eight slides"
- "Summarise this variance analysis for people who do not read financials"
- "Write the email version of this for the founders, they already know about the Pune delay"

Pairs directly with the other skills in this set. Run variance commentary or the cash flow forecast first, then hand the output straight to this one.

## How it protects accuracy

- Every number must trace to your source. It does not introduce figures, estimate to fill gaps, or round in a way that changes the message.
- Anything material that the source does not cover becomes an open item rather than an approximation.
- It will not make a number look better than it is. Presentation is not spin, and a brief that flatters gets you caught once.

## Limits worth knowing

- It presents analysis. It does not create it. Give it the source, and make sure the numbers are final.
- Where a recommendation is a business judgement rather than a finance one, it structures the options and their impact and leaves the call to you.
- It cannot know your reader's preferences or your internal politics. Telling it who reads it and what they already know covers most of that gap.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too generic | It only received totals | Give it the detailed analysis, not the summary. |
| Runs past one page | Too many findings supplied as material | Tell it the top three, or ask it to cut to a page. |
| Wrong emphasis | Reader not specified | Say who reads it and what decision they face. |
| Decisions have no owners | Source did not name any | Tell it who owns what, or it will flag them as unassigned. |
| Too much jargon left in | Reader assumed to be finance-literate | Say the reader is non-finance and rerun. |
