# Operations Review Brief

Turns the month's operations numbers into a one-page brief or a customer escalation: what was committed against what was delivered, what the gap cost, and what changes, with owners and dates.

---

## Who this is for

Anyone who has to stand up and explain operations to people who were not there: operations and supply chain managers, plant and warehouse heads, logistics managers, planning managers, customer service and key account leads handling escalations, and founders reporting to a board or a major customer.

## The problem it solves

Operations gets judged once a month by people who only saw the outcome.

The instinct is to explain everything that happened, in the order it happened, because that is how the month was experienced. But a leader reads for exposure, not chronology. If the first line does not say whether the commitment was met, they build their own conclusion from whatever number they see first, and the rest of the page gets spent correcting it.

Two things then go wrong reliably. Everything gets blamed on something external, which nobody believes and which quietly destroys the credibility of the parts that genuinely were. And containment gets reported as the fix, so the same item appears in three consecutive reviews with a new explanation each time. The distance between a team that fixed the problem and a team that gets credit for fixing it is one page. This writes that page.

---

## What you give it

**Required:** the numbers already worked out. Service level, fill rate, inventory, cost, exceptions, whatever this review runs on. This skill presents analysis; it does not create it.

**Tell it these and the output changes materially:**

| Context | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Who reads it, inside or outside the company** | An internal review and a customer-facing explanation are different documents, and the second carries commercial risk |
| **What was committed, and by whom** | A miss can only be stated against a commitment. Without one there is no story, only a number |
| Whether a decision is being sought, or this is information | A brief written for a decision is structured differently |
| What they already know | Stops the brief opening on last month's news |
| For an escalation, whether a recovery date has already been given | It will not contradict a date you have committed, and it will not invent one you have not |

**Works from:** the inventory health output, the supplier scorecard, the replenishment plan, your own dashboard exports, or notes from your own analysis, as long as the numbers are final.

## What you get back

A one-page brief, roughly 400 to 500 words:

1. **Committed against delivered.** Two or three lines. The commitment, the actual, the gap
2. **What drove the gap.** Three to five items, cause first, number attached, marked controllable or not
3. **What it cost.** In money or in a consequence the reader already tracks
4. **What changes.** Each action with an owner, a date, and how anyone will know it worked
5. **Risks carried forward.** Two to four lines, each with its escalation trigger

Or the same content as a five to eight slide deck, one message per slide, every title written as a sentence. Or a customer-facing version, where the structure holds but internal blame, individual names and uncommitted dates all come out.

## The controllable line, and why it is enforced

This is the section that decides whether the brief is believed, and the one most often abused.

| Controllable | Not controllable |
|---|---|
| A planning error, a late indent, a wrong forecast | A supplier's plant shutdown |
| A picking failure, an unplanned changeover | A port closure or a transporter strike |
| A decision taken too slowly | A customer changing the order after cut-off |

Two rules make it credible. **Every not-controllable item needs evidence attached**, or it moves to controllable. And **every one needs its mitigation stated**, because a genuinely external cause with no mitigation attempted becomes controllable the second time it happens.

A brief in which nothing was controllable is not believed, and it should not be.

## Containment is not a fix

The most common failure in this document, and the reason problems recur.

**Containment** stops the bleeding now: air-freighting the shortfall, borrowing stock from another depot, running an extra shift. **Fix** stops it recurring: changing the reorder point, adding a second source, moving the order cut-off.

Reporting the first as the second is why the same item comes back next month. The output labels them separately and will not let a containment action stand in for a fix.

## Translation, with the consequence attached

The reader thinks in customers, orders, cash and risk. Operations metrics have to be converted, and the conversion has to carry the consequence:

| What operations says | What the room needs to hear |
|---|---|
| "Fill rate fell to 91 percent" | "38 customer orders shipped short, 11 of them to the three accounts up for renewal" |
| "Inventory days rose to 74" | "18 days more stock than plan, which is 2.1 crore of cash, most of it slow-moving electricals" |
| "Supplier OTIF is 62 percent" | "That supplier missed a third of its commitments, and it is our only source for four of our top ten items" |

The consequence is the half operations leaves out and the half that gets the problem resourced.

## What good output looks like

A strong brief answers the question in its first line and fits on a page. Every number traces to your source, every action has a named owner and a date, and the bad news sits in the first half rather than the last paragraph.

The controllable column should not be empty. A brief attributing the whole month to external causes reads as defensive even when it is accurate, and it discounts everything else you write. If it comes back generic you supplied a summary rather than the analysis; if it runs past a page, too much was treated as material, so name your top three and rerun.

## Using it

Paste or attach the analysis and say who is reading it.

Examples that work:

- "Turn this into the one-page ops review for Thursday. We committed 96 percent fill rate and hit 91."
- "Customer escalation. We missed their launch stock. They have not been given a recovery date yet."
- "Eight slides for the management review. Here are the numbers and here is what we already fixed."
- "The same picking issue is in this review for the third month. Help me write it honestly."

Saying what was committed and by whom is the input that changes the output most.

## Limits worth knowing

- It presents analysis, it does not create it. Give it the numbers and make sure they are final.
- It does not commit a recovery date, a delivery date or a service level on your behalf. Dates come from whoever has to deliver them.
- It does not accept, assign or deny liability, and it does not interpret a penalty clause. Those are contract and legal questions.
- It will not estimate a penalty or a lost-revenue figure. Where the cost is unknown it says so and names who could size it.
- It does not attribute a cause to a supplier, a team or a person without the evidence sitting in your source.
- It cannot know your internal politics. Telling it who reads it, and what they already know, covers most of that gap.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reads generic | It received a summary, not the analysis | Give it the detail. It compresses; it cannot expand. |
| Runs past one page | Too many items treated as material | Name your top three drivers and rerun. |
| Nothing in the controllable column | Causes supplied without evidence or mitigation | It moves unevidenced external causes to controllable. Supply the evidence or accept the classification. |
| An action has no owner | The source did not name one | It flags them as unassigned rather than inventing an owner. |
| A recovery date is missing | Nobody has committed to one | Deliberate. It will not invent a date a customer can hold you to. |
| Cost of the miss shows as unknown | Not in the source | It names who could size it, which is more useful than an estimate. |
