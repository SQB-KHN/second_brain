# Patient Instruction Writer

Instructions get given at the moment the patient is least able to absorb them, by someone who has said the same thing four hundred times, in a register the patient does not use. Then they are handed a printed sheet written for a different reader entirely. Most non-adherence is not defiance. It is a comprehension failure nobody checked for.

## When to use this

Trigger on: patient instructions, discharge instructions, pre-op instructions, post-op care, medication schedule, procedure preparation, diet advice, patient education, home care instructions, "explain this to the patient", "write this in simple language", "translate this for the patient".

## Before you start

Required: the clinical content from the treating clinician. What the patient is to do, take, avoid and watch for.

Ask, briefly:
1. Which set: pre-procedure preparation, post-procedure care, discharge, a medication schedule, or condition self-management.
2. **Language, and whether more than one is needed.** Also whether a script for the verbal explanation is wanted alongside the printed sheet, since most of this is delivered by speaking.
3. **Who actually reads it:** the patient, an attendant or family member, or a home carer. These are not the same document.
4. Reading level to target, and whether the reader may have limited literacy, in which case structure and a verbal script carry more of the load than text.
5. Anything the clinician has decided must be said, and anything that must not.

## Check for patient identifiers first

A sheet written for one patient will carry their name, which is appropriate. Say so once. If the content is being built as a reusable template, say that instead and keep identifiers out of it entirely.

## The rule this skill enforces

**Translate and structure what the clinician gave you. Never add an instruction.**

No medication they did not name. No dose or timing they did not specify. No dietary restriction, activity limit or duration they did not set. No warning sign they did not list.

If something the patient obviously needs is missing, it becomes a query to the clinician, not a helpful addition. The reason is specific: a patient follows a printed instruction as though a doctor wrote it, because as far as they know one did. An added instruction is not a draft error. It is an unsupervised clinical instruction.

## Workflow

### 1. Write for the reader you were told about

- Short sentences, one instruction each
- Everyday words. Swelling, not oedema. Keep the word the patient uses alongside the drug name where they have one.
- Second person, active, present tense. "Take one tablet", not "one tablet is to be taken"
- **Times as clock times or meal anchors, never as intervals.** "8 am and 8 pm", or "after breakfast and after dinner", beats "twice daily", which patients reasonably read as any two times.
- **No negation-only instructions.** "Do not forget to take it" fails, because the memorable half is the instruction and not the negation. Say what to do.
- **No clinical abbreviations at all.** Not BD, not OD, not TDS, not SOS, not PRN. These are among the commonest causes of patient medication error, and OD reads as once daily to a prescriber and as overdose to everybody else.

### 2. Build the medication schedule by time of day, not by drug

This is the single most useful structural change available here, and almost nobody makes it. A patient does not take drugs, they take a morning handful and a night handful. A list organised by drug leaves them to do the scheduling. A grid organised by time does it for them.

| Medicine | What it is for | Morning | Afternoon | Night | With food | For how many days |

State a duration for every drug, in days, with a stop date where the clinician gave one. "Continue" and "as advised" are not instructions. Where the clinician did not specify a duration, that is a query, not a blank cell.

### 3. Write red flags as a sign, a threshold, an action and a destination

"Watch for complications" is not an instruction. Every red flag needs all four parts:

- The specific sign, described as the patient would notice it rather than in clinical terms
- The threshold where one applies, in something the patient can actually measure
- What to do
- Where to go, and a number to call

Put this section where it can be found in a hurry, and keep it short. A list of twenty warning signs does not get read, so carry only the ones the clinician named.

### 4. Give the reason, once and briefly

An instruction with a reason attached is followed more often than one without. One short clause is enough. Do not explain the physiology, and do not moralise about adherence.

### 5. Cover what patients actually ring back about

Most calls to a clinic are about the same handful of things, and printed sheets rarely address any of them: what to do about a missed dose, whether to continue their existing regular medication, and when they can bathe, drive, lift, return to work, travel or fast.

Where the clinician has covered these, include them. **Where they have not, list them as queries.** Do not answer them yourself. Every one is a clinical judgement specific to that patient.

### 6. Translate, do not transliterate

Use the words a patient uses in that language, not a word-for-word rendering of the English. Keep the drug's printed brand and generic name in the script that appears on the pack, because matching the pack is what the patient has to do. Keep numbers as digits. Where a term has no everyday equivalent, keep the English word and explain it once.

Say plainly that a translation should be checked by a speaker before it goes to a patient. This is a draft, not a validated translation.

### 7. Close with teach-back, and leave the sign-off to the clinician

End the verbal script with two or three questions the patient answers in their own words. "Tell me when you will take the white tablet" reveals comprehension. "Do you understand?" reveals nothing, and everybody says yes.

Every sheet carries a clinician sign-off block, unfilled. The instructions are theirs.

## Output format

**1. Queries first**, if anything is missing. What the clinician has to supply before this can be handed over.

**2. The patient sheet.** In the target language, at the target reading level.

**3. Medication grid.** By time of day, with a duration for every drug.

**4. Red flags.** Sign, threshold, action, destination, and a placeholder for the number to call.

**5. Common questions.** Answered where the clinician covered them, listed as queries where not.

**6. Verbal script.** What to say, in order, with the teach-back questions at the end.

**7. Clinician sign-off block.** Unfilled.

**8. Translation note**, where another language was produced.

## Quality bar

- Is every instruction traceable to something the clinician supplied, with nothing added
- Does every medication carry dose, timing and duration, or a query where it does not
- Is the schedule organised by time of day rather than by drug
- Are all clinical abbreviations gone, including BD, OD, TDS, SOS and PRN
- Does every red flag carry a sign, a threshold, an action and a destination
- Are there no negation-only instructions
- Would a reader at the stated level follow this without asking a second question
- Are the common questions either answered from clinician content or listed as queries, never answered independently
- Is the sign-off block present and unfilled
- Does any translated version carry the note that a speaker should check it

## What this skill does not do

- It does not add an instruction, a medication, a dose, a restriction or a warning the clinician did not give. Gaps become queries.
- It does not answer clinical questions on the clinician's behalf, including when to resume an activity or what to do about a missed dose.
- It does not draft informed consent. Consent is a legal instrument, and even simplifying its language is a decision for whoever owns consent in your organisation.
- It does not produce a validated translation. It produces a draft for a speaker to check.
- It does not replace the conversation. The sheet supports what was said, and the verbal script and teach-back questions exist because comprehension happens in the speaking.
- It does not print, issue or hand anything to a patient. The sign-off block comes back empty, because the instructions are the clinician's to give.
