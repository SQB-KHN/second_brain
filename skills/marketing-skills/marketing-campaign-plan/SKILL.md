# Campaign Plan

Most campaign plans are a list of channels and a budget. The two things that decide whether a campaign works are missing: whether the numbers can possibly reach the target, and what result means stop. Both have to be settled before launch, because afterwards nobody wants to kill their own campaign.

## When to use this

Trigger on: campaign plan, launch plan, media plan, budget allocation, "we have X to spend", go-to-market plan, "what channels should we use".

## Before you start

Required: an objective, a budget and a timeline.

Ask for these. The first is not optional.

1. **The objective, as a number with a date.** Awareness is not an objective. Push for 200 qualified leads by 30 November, or 50 new customers this quarter. If the user cannot give a number, help them derive one from a business target, and say plainly that a campaign without a number cannot be judged and will therefore run forever.
2. **Budget**, and whether it is fixed or has room to scale if something works.
3. **Timeline**, including any fixed date the campaign is tied to.
4. **Audience**, and whether it is cold, warm, or existing customers. These need entirely different plans.
5. **What exists already**, such as a landing page, creative, a list, previous campaign results. Previous results are the most valuable input available, since they replace assumptions with the user's own numbers.

**One objective per campaign.** If several are named, say that multiple objectives means no objective, and ask which one they would keep if forced to choose.

## Workflow

### 1. Do the funnel maths before anything else

Work backwards from the objective. This is the step that saves the most money, because it frequently shows the plan cannot work before a rupee is spent.

```
Target outcome
÷ conversion rate at each stage
= volume needed at the top
× cost per unit at the top
= budget required
```

Use the user's own historical rates wherever they have them. Where they do not, use a clearly labelled assumption and say it is an assumption. Never present an assumed conversion rate as though it were a benchmark.

Then compare budget required against budget available and say plainly which of these is true:

- The maths works with room to spare
- The maths works only if every rate holds, which is fragile
- The maths does not work, and the target, budget or timeline has to change

If it is the third, say so first and clearly. A plan that quietly assumes a doubling of conversion rate is not a plan.

### 2. Choose channels by audience and objective, not by habit

For each channel, state why it is in the plan: where the audience already is, what the objective needs, and what it costs to reach them there.

Be specific about the split and the reasoning behind it. A generic even split across four channels usually means no decision was made.

Practical notes worth applying:

- Cold audiences need volume and time before anything can be judged. Warm and retargeting audiences convert faster but cannot scale beyond the pool that exists, so a retargeting-heavy plan hits a ceiling quickly.
- Owned channels, email and messaging, usually carry the best economics and are usually underweighted in plans.
- Include only channels the team can actually execute in the time available. A plan requiring video the team cannot produce is not a plan.

### 3. Write the message for each funnel stage

The most common campaign failure is running one message at every stage.

- **Cold:** the problem. They do not know they need you, so leading with your differentiation lands on nobody.
- **Warm:** the differentiation. They know the problem, they are choosing between options.
- **Hot:** the offer and the risk reversal. They are convinced and looking for a reason not to hesitate.

Pull these from the positioning brief if one exists rather than inventing new claims here.

### 4. Specify creative as requirements, not as ideas

State how many variants, what each one tests, and what stays constant. Testing three creatives that differ in every respect teaches nothing, because there is no way to know which difference mattered.

Note the minimum volume each variant needs before it can be judged, and say plainly that variants which have not reached it should not be paused on early numbers.

### 5. Set measurement before launch

- **One primary metric**, tied directly to the objective
- **Guardrail metrics**, things that must not get worse while the primary improves. Cost per acquisition rising while volume rises is the classic one, and without a guardrail it goes unnoticed for weeks.
- **Reporting cadence**, and the date of the first review, which should be after enough volume has accumulated to mean anything rather than after two days
- **What is not being measured**, stated honestly, so nobody claims it later

### 6. Agree kill criteria now

This is the section everyone skips and the one that saves the most money.

Write down, before launch: what number, by what date, means stop. Also what number means scale.

Set it now because after launch nobody wants to kill their own campaign, and every underperforming campaign gets one more week, repeatedly, until the budget is gone.

Make the criteria specific enough that two people would read them the same way. "If CPA is above 900 rupees after 500 clicks, pause and rework the landing page" is a kill criterion. "If it is not working we will review" is not.

### 7. Phase the budget

- Test budget first, sized to reach the minimum volume needed for a decision
- Named trigger for scaling, and the rate at which scaling happens
- Reserve held back, so there is something left to spend on whatever turns out to work

## Output format

**1. Objective.** Number and date.

**2. Funnel maths.** Each stage, the rate used, and whether it is historical or assumed. Ending with a plain verdict on whether the budget can reach the target.

**3. Channel plan.** Split, with the reason for each channel and what it is responsible for.

**4. Message by stage.** Cold, warm, hot.

**5. Creative requirements.** Variant count, what each tests, minimum volume before judging.

**6. Measurement.** Primary metric, guardrails, cadence, first review date, what is not measured.

**7. Kill and scale criteria.** Specific numbers, specific dates.

**8. Budget phasing.** Test, trigger, scale, reserve.

**9. Assumptions and risks.** Every assumed rate, listed, with what happens if it is wrong.

## Quality bar

- Is the objective a number with a date
- Does the funnel maths use historical rates where they exist, with every assumption labelled
- Has the plan stated clearly whether the budget can actually reach the target
- Does every channel have a stated reason beyond being popular
- Are kill criteria specific enough that two people would read them identically
- Is there exactly one primary metric
- Would the team be able to execute this with the people and time they have

## What this skill does not do

- It does not promise a result. It shows what would have to be true, and says when the maths does not work.
- It does not write the creative or the copy.
- It does not buy media or set up campaigns.
- It does not invent benchmark conversion rates and present them as fact. Assumed rates are labelled as assumptions every time.
