# Positioning and Messaging Brief

Builds the messaging document every other marketing output depends on, with proof behind every claim and a do-not-say list.

---

## Who this is for

Marketers who own what the company says about itself: brand and product marketers, growth leads, founders doing their own marketing, and agency strategists who rebuild this from scratch for every client.

## The problem it solves

Positioning is a decision document, not copy. Everything produced afterwards inherits whatever was decided here.

Teams that skip it spend the next year arguing about taglines, because there is no agreed answer to appeal to. Teams that do it badly end up with a page of superlatives that any competitor could have written, which is worse, because it looks finished.

This produces the document, and refuses to let a claim through without proof sitting behind it. Two to four hours of work, done properly once, and it stops being redone every quarter.

---

## What you give it

**It will ask five things, and the first three matter most:**

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| What the product actually does | In plain terms, as if explaining to someone outside your industry |
| Who buys it and who uses it | Often different people who need different messages |
| What they use today instead | Frequently a spreadsheet, a junior employee, or nothing. Most teams name a competitor when the real alternative is inertia. |
| What competitors claim | Their actual website words, not your summary of them |
| What proof you hold | Customer results, data, demonstrable capability, third-party validation |

**Helpful if you have it:** customer research, win and loss notes, competitor pages pasted in, your current website copy.

## What you get back

1. A positioning statement in a fixed structure
2. Audience defined by situation, with buyer and user separated where they differ
3. The problem, written in customer language
4. The real alternative, named honestly
5. **A differentiation table** with the proof and proof type behind every claim
6. Message hierarchy: one primary, three supporting, per segment where needed
7. **A do-not-say list**
8. **Claims you cannot make yet**, each with the evidence that would unlock it
9. Open questions, and who inside your organisation can answer them

---

## The two tests it runs

**The table stakes test.** For every proposed differentiator, could a competitor claim the same thing? If yes, it is a category requirement, not a differentiator. Most things teams call differentiators fail this, and it is better to find out here than in a sales call.

**The opposite test.** Would any competitor claim the reverse? If nobody would ever say "we are slow, expensive and hard to use", then "fast, affordable and easy" says nothing at all. Positioning nobody would contest is positioning nobody will remember.

## What good output looks like

A strong brief is uncomfortable to read. Half your current claims move to table stakes, one or two go on the cannot-make-yet list, and the do-not-say list contains phrases your website uses today. The positioning statement should be something a competitor would actually argue with.

If the brief agrees with everything you already say and the do-not-say list is empty, it has not done its job. That usually means the inputs were thin, particularly the competitor wording.

## The claim it will not let through

Every claim needs a named proof: data you hold, customer evidence, something demonstrable, or third-party validation.

Anything without proof does not enter the brief. It moves to a list of claims you cannot make yet, along with the evidence needed to unlock it. That list often becomes the most useful page in the document, because it turns into your research plan.

It will not invent a statistic, a customer quote or a market position to fill a gap. Unsubstantiated superlatives are a real exposure under advertising standards in most markets, and they are the fastest way to lose a buyer who checks.

## Using it

Describe the product and the market. Paste competitor copy if you have it.

Examples that work:

- "Build our positioning brief. We sell inventory software to small manufacturers, here is what our competitors say."
- "We sound identical to everyone in our category. Work out what we can actually claim."
- "New product launching next month, I need the messaging framework before the website gets written."
- "Here is our current homepage. Pull it apart and tell me what we cannot substantiate."

## Limits worth knowing

- It does not write copy. It produces the document copy gets written from.
- It does not invent proof, statistics, quotes or market positions.
- It does not decide pricing or product strategy, though it will flag where your positioning and your product disagree.
- It does not clear anything legally. Flagged claims go to whoever owns that in your organisation.
- The output is only as good as the competitor input. Paste their actual words rather than summarising them.

## If something looks wrong

| What you see | Usual cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning feels generic | Real alternative not identified, or inputs were thin | Say what customers actually do today instead of buying from you. |
| Most claims moved to table stakes | Working as intended | That is the finding. Push on what is genuinely only true of you. |
| Cannot-make-yet list is long | Little proof supplied | It becomes your research plan. Each item names the evidence needed. |
| Too many primary messages | Several genuine audiences | Ask for a message set per audience rather than one averaged message. |
| It keeps asking questions | Product description too abstract | Describe what it does in plain words, without category language. |
