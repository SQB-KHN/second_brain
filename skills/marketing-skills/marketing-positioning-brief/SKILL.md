# Positioning and Messaging Brief

Positioning is a decision document, not copy. Everything a marketing team produces afterwards inherits whatever was decided here, which is why teams that skip it spend the next year arguing about taglines. This skill produces the document, and refuses to let a claim through without proof behind it.

## When to use this

Trigger on: positioning, messaging framework, value proposition, how do we differentiate, launch messaging, "what should our website say", rebrand, "we sound the same as everyone else".

## Before you start

Required: a plain description of what the product does and who buys it.

Ask these, and do not proceed on guesses for the first three.

1. **What does the product actually do?** In the plainest possible terms, as if explaining to someone outside the industry.
2. **Who buys it, and who uses it?** These are often different people and need different messages.
3. **What do they use today instead?** The honest answer is frequently a spreadsheet, a junior employee, or nothing at all. Naming the real alternative changes the entire brief, and most teams name a competitor when the true alternative is inertia.
4. **What do competitors claim?** Their actual website words, not a summary.
5. **What proof exists?** Customer results, data, capabilities that can be demonstrated, third-party validation.

## The two tests that do most of the work

**The table stakes test.** For every proposed differentiator, ask whether a competitor could claim the same thing. If they could, it is a category requirement, not a differentiator. Most things teams call differentiators fail this. Say so plainly and move them to a separate table stakes list, because they still need saying, just not in the headline.

**The opposite test.** Would any sane competitor claim the reverse of your positioning? If nobody would ever say "we are slow, expensive and hard to use", then "fast, affordable and easy" says nothing. Positioning that no one would contest is positioning that no one will remember.

Run both before writing the statement. A brief that fails these two is a brief that will be quietly ignored.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the audience narrowly

Push back on "small and medium businesses" or "working professionals". A useful audience definition includes the situation they are in, not just who they are. "Finance managers at 50 to 200 person manufacturing companies who close books manually in Excel" is workable. "SMBs" is not.

If the honest answer is that several distinct audiences exist, say so and build a message set per audience rather than averaging them into a message that fits none.

### 2. Name the problem in their words, not the category's words

The problem statement should be something the customer would recognise and say themselves. If it uses a term they would not use, it is the industry's problem statement, not theirs.

### 3. Establish the real alternative

Position against what they actually do today. If most prospects currently do nothing, the competition is inertia and the brief must address why now, not why us. This single distinction changes what the whole campaign has to argue.

### 4. Substantiate every claim, one by one

This is the core discipline. For each claim, record the proof and its type:

- **Data you hold**, with the figure and where it comes from
- **Customer evidence**, with who said it and whether they will be named
- **Demonstrable capability**, something a prospect can be shown
- **Third-party validation**, with the source

A claim with no proof does not go in the brief. It goes on a separate list called **claims we cannot make yet**, along with what evidence would be needed to unlock it. That list is often the most useful page in the document, because it becomes the research plan.

Never invent a proof point, a statistic, a customer quote or a market position. If a claim needs a number and the user does not have one, the claim stays on the cannot-make-yet list. An unsubstantiated superlative is a real exposure under advertising standards in most markets, and it is also the fastest way for a brand to lose credibility with a buyer who checks.

### 5. Build the message hierarchy

- One primary message. Not two. If everything is primary, nothing is.
- Three supporting messages that each earn their place by handling a different objection or audience concern
- Per audience segment where segments genuinely differ

### 6. Write the do-not-say list

Every brief needs one, and it is the section teams actually refer back to:

- Claims that failed substantiation
- Superlatives with no evidence: best, leading, number one, world-class, revolutionary
- Competitor comparisons that cannot be backed with a source
- Category words that have stopped meaning anything: solution, platform, seamless, innovative, cutting-edge, game-changing
- Anything legal or compliance has previously flagged, if the user knows of any

## Output format

**1. Positioning statement.**

```
For [audience in their situation]
who [problem in their words],
[product] is the [category]
that [primary benefit].
Unlike [the real alternative],
it [differentiation, with proof].
```

**2. Audience.** Defined by situation, with buyer and user separated where they differ.

**3. The problem.** In their language.

**4. The real alternative.** Including inertia if that is the honest answer.

**5. Differentiation table.**

| Claim | Proof | Proof type | Table stakes or differentiator |

**6. Message hierarchy.** Primary, three supporting, per segment where needed.

**7. Do-not-say list.**

**8. Claims we cannot make yet.** Each with the evidence that would unlock it.

**9. Open questions.** What the user could not answer, and who inside their organisation can.

## Quality bar

There are no arithmetic checks here, so judge the output against these instead. Every one should be answerable yes.

- Would a competitor contest this positioning, rather than agreeing with it
- Does every claim in the differentiation table have a named proof
- Is the audience defined by situation rather than by category
- Is the real alternative named honestly, including the possibility that it is inertia
- Would a customer recognise the problem statement as their own words
- Is there exactly one primary message
- Does the do-not-say list contain something the team currently says

If the last one is empty, the brief has probably not changed anything.

## What this skill does not do

- It does not write copy. It produces the document copy is written from.
- It does not invent proof, statistics, quotes or market positions.
- It does not decide your pricing or product strategy, though it will flag where positioning and product disagree.
- It does not clear anything legally. Flagged claims go to whoever owns that in your organisation.
