# Customer Language Synthesis

Copy sounds generic because it is written in the company's vocabulary rather than the customer's. The fix is not better writing. It is having the customer's actual words in front of you before you write anything. This skill extracts them from material most companies already have and never read systematically.

## When to use this

Trigger on: voice of customer, customer research, "our copy sounds generic", review analysis, what objections do we face, message testing, "what words do customers use".

## Before you start

Required: raw customer text. Reviews of your product or a competitor's, survey open-ends, support tickets, sales call notes or transcripts, chat logs, social comments, win and loss interviews.

Ask:
1. Which source each file is, since each carries a different bias and the output must account for it
2. Whether these are your customers, a competitor's, or the category generally. Competitor reviews are often more useful, because they describe unmet needs.
3. What the output is for, since copy, positioning research and product feedback need different emphasis

## Check for personal data first

Customer text routinely contains names, email addresses, phone numbers, order numbers and account details.

Say so once if you find any, and ask whether the user is permitted to share the file. Offer to work with those fields removed, since this analysis needs the language, not the identity. Then continue with whatever they decide. State it once and do not repeat it.

## Name the bias of each source before analysing it

Every source is skewed, and an output that ignores this misleads. State the skew plainly at the top:

- **Public reviews** skew to extremes. Delighted and furious customers write reviews. The satisfied middle does not.
- **Support tickets** skew to problems by definition. They tell you what breaks, not what people love.
- **Sales call notes** are filtered through the salesperson's memory and interest.
- **Surveys** skew to whoever bothers to respond, and the question wording shapes the answer.
- **Win and loss interviews** are the richest source and the rarest.

## Workflow

### 1. Extract verbatim, never paraphrase

This is the entire point of the skill, so protect it.

Pull the customer's actual words, including awkward phrasing, incomplete sentences and imperfect grammar. The moment a phrase is tidied up it becomes the company's voice again and the value is gone.

Every extracted phrase must trace to a source row. Never write a quote that is not in the data. If a phrase is edited at all, even for length, mark it.

### 2. Sort what you extract into these buckets

- **Problems**, described the way they described them
- **Outcomes wanted**, what they were trying to achieve, which is often different from the feature they asked for
- **Trigger events**, what made them start looking. This is the most commercially useful and most overlooked category, because it tells you when to reach someone.
- **Objections and hesitations**, including the ones raised after purchase
- **Alternatives considered**, what they compared against and how they described the comparison
- **Praise**, specifically what they say the product let them stop doing
- **Complaints**, separated into product issues and expectation mismatches, which need completely different responses

### 3. Rank by frequency, not by how well phrased

A vivid one-off quote is tempting and misleading. Count mentions, state the count, and rank on it. A phrase used by thirty customers matters more than an eloquent complaint from one.

Where the volume is large enough, compute the counts in code rather than estimating them.

### 4. Build the vocabulary gap table

This is usually the highest-value output. Two columns:

| What the company says | What customers say |

Companies say solution, platform, streamline, optimise. Customers say the thing that stops me chasing invoices every month. The gap is where generic copy comes from, and seeing it side by side changes writing faster than any amount of advice.

### 5. Keep evidence and interpretation apart

- **Verbatims are evidence.** They are what the customer said.
- **Themes are your interpretation.** They are what you concluded from a group of verbatims.

Label them separately and never let a theme be presented as though a customer said it. Under each theme, show the verbatims it rests on and the count. If a theme rests on two mentions, it is a hypothesis, not a theme, and should be labelled that way.

### 6. Say what the data does not cover

List the questions this material cannot answer, and which source would answer them. Support tickets cannot tell you why people chose you. Reviews cannot tell you why people left without buying. Naming the gap stops someone over-reading the findings.

## Output format

**1. Sources and their bias.** What was analysed, how much, and what each source skews toward.

**2. Vocabulary gap table.**

**3. Problems, ranked by mention count**, each with two or three verbatims underneath.

**4. Trigger events.** What made people start looking.

**5. Objections, ranked**, with the verbatims and the count.

**6. Alternatives mentioned**, and how customers described them.

**7. Swipe file.** The verbatim phrases strong enough to use directly in copy, grouped by where they would fit. Marked clearly as customer words, so anyone using them knows to keep them intact.

**8. Themes.** Labelled as interpretation, each with its supporting evidence and count.

**9. What this data cannot tell you.** With the source that would.

## Quality bar

- Is every quote traceable to a source row, with nothing invented
- Is every phrase in the swipe file in the customer's own words rather than tidied
- Does every theme show its count and its supporting verbatims
- Is the bias of each source stated at the top rather than buried
- Are themes resting on fewer than three mentions labelled as hypotheses
- Does the vocabulary gap table contain words the company genuinely uses today

## What this skill does not do

- It does not invent or improve quotes. Tidied language defeats the purpose.
- It does not tell you your customers are representative. It tells you what this sample said and how it is skewed.
- It does not write the copy. It gives you the raw material that makes copy sound human.
- It cannot analyse text you do not have. It will tell you which source would answer the questions this one cannot.
